# Backward Causal Reasoning

**Placement:** Synochi → General reasoning layer**Status:** Proposed node; existing coverage not verified.

## **Definition**

A reasoning method that starts from an observed outcome and works backwards through preceding events, decisions and conditions to identify plausible contributing causes.

## **Purpose**

Explain how an outcome developed and identify where a change could influence future outcomes. Applies to successful, unsuccessful and unexpected results.

## **Operating Sequence**

1. **Define the outcome.** Separate what happened from the interpretation attached to it.
2. **Reconstruct preceding events.** Identify relevant actions, conditions and interactions; mark gaps in the evidence.
3. **Generate competing explanations.** Consider multiple contributors rather than assuming a single cause.
4. **Compare cases.** Look for recurring contributors and cases where similar conditions produced different outcomes.
5. **Evaluate causal support.** Ask whether each proposed contributor plausibly influenced the outcome, rather than merely preceding it.
6. **Identify a test.** Specify what observation or feasible change would strengthen or weaken the explanation.
7. **Update the conclusion.** Revise confidence as evidence accumulates.

## **Core Principles**

- Temporal order alone does not establish causation.
- An outcome can have multiple interacting causes.
- Different pathways can produce the same outcome.
- The same action can produce different outcomes under different conditions.
- Earlier causes and immediate triggers serve different explanatory roles.
- The earliest contributor is not automatically the best intervention point.
- Missing evidence must remain visible.
- Successful counterexamples help distinguish causal contributors from incidental features.
- Counterfactual claims—“changing this would have changed the outcome”—remain hypotheses until adequately supported.

## **Output**

- Clearly defined outcome.
- Relevant preceding sequence or branching paths.
- Candidate contributors and supporting evidence.
- Alternative explanations and counterexamples.
- Uncertainty and missing information.
- A testable next step.

## **Boundary**

Backward Causal Reasoning generates and evaluates causal hypotheses. It does not establish a cause solely through a plausible narrative or repeated association.

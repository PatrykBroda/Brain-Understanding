Definition

My cortical homunculus is the internal body-map in my brain that shows how much sensory processing power is assigned to each part of my body.

It’s not a picture — it’s a distribution of sensory priority.

  

Some areas of me (face, hands, jaw, tongue) take up far more neural space than others (back, legs, torso).

---

### What It Means In My System

  

Because certain body parts have higher sensory representation, they act as strong anchors for awareness and regulation.

  

That’s why:

  

• jaw work changes my whole state

• facial relaxation affects perception

• hand and foot awareness stabilise attention

• subtle face tension impacts threat sensitivity

  

I’m not just feeling these areas more.

My brain literally gives them more processing power.

---

### Relationship to Sensory Anchoring

  

When I use sensory anchoring, I’m exploiting this built-in mapping.

  

Anchoring through:

  

• jaw

• face

• breath

• hands

• feet

  

is powerful because those areas occupy more neural territory in the somatosensory cortex.

  

The cortical homunculus is the biological substrate that makes sensory anchoring effective.

---

### How I Apply This Practically

  

I use high-representation zones for anchoring because:

  

• they stabilise my state faster

• they give clearer feedback

• they reduce cognitive effort

• they influence deep nervous system layers

  

This is why jaw, facial and breath work sit so high in my hierarchy.

---

### Why This Node Matters

  

Without this, sensory anchoring sounds theoretical.

  

With this:

  

• it becomes neurologically grounded

• it explains why certain anchors work better

• it connects my subjective practice to actual brain architecture

• it justifies why the face and jaw are central in my model

  

It gives structural legitimacy to what I’m already experiencing.

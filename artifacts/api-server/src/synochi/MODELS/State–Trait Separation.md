
## **Core Idea**

Synochi should distinguish between:

**State** — what is happening to the person now.

and:

**Trait** — a relatively stable pattern that persists across time and contexts.

Temporary states should not be allowed to become permanent identity conclusions.

The key question is:

Is this a stable characteristic, or is it a temporary expression produced by current conditions?

## **State**

A state is temporary and context-sensitive.

Examples include:

- fatigue,
- stress,
- low confidence,
- emotional disruption,
- high arousal,
- poor sleep,
- cognitive overload,
- temporary motivation loss,
- pain,
- unusually high readiness.

States can strongly affect behaviour without accurately describing the person as a whole.

## **Trait**

A trait is a more persistent pattern that appears repeatedly across time and relevant contexts.

Examples might include:

- consistently preferring high autonomy,
- repeatedly responding well to direct feedback,
- persistently favouring fast decision-making,
- showing the same learning preference across environments.

Traits should require stronger and broader evidence than states.

## **Avoiding Identity Errors**

Synochi should avoid conclusions such as:

“The athlete is unconfident.”

when the evidence only supports:

“The athlete currently has low confidence.”

Likewise:

“The user is indecisive.”

may actually mean:

“Decision-making slows when uncertainty is high and structure is weak.”

State should not become identity by accident.

## **Evidence Requirement**

A trait should require:

- repetition,
- persistence over time,
- appearance across multiple relevant contexts,
- and evidence that the pattern remains even when temporary states change.

A single event, difficult week, competition loss, emotional disruption, or poor training block should rarely justify a trait-level conclusion.

## **State Can Reveal Trait**

States and traits are not completely separate.

A temporary state may expose a stable underlying pattern.

For example:

Under severe fatigue, the athlete repeatedly narrows attention too aggressively.

The fatigue is a state.

The repeated response pattern may eventually reveal a trait-like tendency.

Synochi should separate the trigger from the recurring response.

## **Trait Can Influence State**

Stable patterns can also shape temporary states.

For example:

A user who strongly values control may experience greater stress when circumstances become unpredictable.

The trait does not equal the state, but it may help explain why the state emerges.

## **Context Matters**

A pattern that looks stable may actually be context-specific.

If confidence only drops in competition, this should not automatically become:

“Low-confidence athlete.”

It may instead be:

“Confidence drops under high-consequence competition conditions.”

This should be interpreted alongside [[Context-Dependent Truth]].

## **Updating the Model**

When new evidence appears, Synochi should ask:

- Is this temporary?
- Is it repeating?
- Does it generalise?
- Does it disappear when the context changes?
- Has this persisted long enough to justify a more stable model belief?

Trait confidence should increase slowly.

State recognition should update quickly.

## **Guidance Implications**

State and trait require different interventions.

If the problem is a temporary state:

regulate, recover, simplify, stabilise, or change the immediate environment.

If the problem reflects a stable pattern:

build longer-term strategies, habits, skills, or environmental structures around it.

Misclassifying one as the other can produce poor guidance.

## **Principle**

**Do not confuse what someone is experiencing with who they are.**

Synochi should model:

**Current State ≠ Stable Trait**

unless repeated evidence shows otherwise.

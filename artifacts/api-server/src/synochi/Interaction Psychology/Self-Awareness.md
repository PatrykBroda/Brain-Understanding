## Definition
Self-Awareness is the ability to notice and accurately reflect on one’s own internal state, behaviour, patterns, reactions, limitations, and decision processes.

Within Synochi, self-awareness is not treated as a personality trait.

It is treated as an information source.

An athlete who can recognise what they are feeling, doing, avoiding, forcing, or repeatedly misunderstanding gives FRAME better evidence for coaching.

## Core Principle
Better self-awareness improves the quality of the model.

But self-report is not automatically truth.

An athlete may accurately notice:

- tension
- hesitation
- confidence
- fatigue
- frustration
- recurring technical failure
- loss of focus
- changes in timing

while still misinterpreting why it is happening.

Synochi should therefore separate:

**observation**

from

**explanation.**

Example:

“I keep freezing when they pressure forward.”

may be valuable evidence.

“I freeze because I’m bad under pressure.”

is an interpretation that still requires testing.

## Self-Awareness as Signal
FRAME should treat self-observation as one input among several.

Useful signals include:

- “I noticed I rushed that.”
- “I felt myself hesitate.”
- “I always lose position when I start forcing.”
- “I was calmer that round.”
- “I stopped seeing the opening once I got tired.”
- “I knew what I wanted to do but couldn’t commit.”

These statements help expose internal processes that may not be visible from outcomes alone.

## Observation vs Narrative
Synochi should distinguish between:

### Direct Observation
What the athlete actually noticed.

Examples:

- “My breathing got faster.”
- “I stopped attacking.”
- “I kept looking for the same pass.”

### Narrative
The meaning the athlete assigns to it.

Examples:

- “I’m mentally weak.”
- “I can’t perform under pressure.”
- “That position just doesn’t suit me.”

Narratives may be useful hypotheses, but should not automatically become model truths.

## Interaction With Ambiguity
[[Ambiguity]] often increases when an athlete notices something but cannot explain it.

FRAME should not force an immediate explanation.

Instead:

notice signal
→ clarify what was actually observed
→ preserve competing explanations
→ test through future behaviour

Self-awareness can reduce ambiguity without prematurely eliminating it.

## Interaction With Intuition
[[Intuition Capture Loop]] becomes relevant when the athlete senses that something is wrong or changing before they can clearly articulate why.

Self-awareness allows those signals to enter the system.

FRAME should help the athlete turn:

“I don’t know, it just felt off”

into something more observable.

For example:

- timing felt late
- decisions felt slower
- position felt unstable
- confidence dropped after one exchange
- effort increased without improved output

The intuition remains evidence, not conclusion.

## Progressive Discovery
[[Progressive Discovery]] should be used when the athlete has partial awareness.

FRAME should not interrogate them with ten questions.

Instead, identify the highest-value next question.

Example:

Athlete:
“My passing felt terrible.”

FRAME:
“Was the problem getting into your passes, or finishing once you got there?”

This converts vague self-report into useful structure.

## Agency Preservation
[[Agency Preservation]] is important because FRAME should not tell the athlete what they are feeling.

Prefer:

“You’ve mentioned hesitation in the last few sessions. Does that feel accurate?”

rather than:

“You’re afraid to commit.”

The athlete should retain authority over their internal experience.

FRAME can detect patterns, but should present them as observations or hypotheses.

## Coaching Application
Self-awareness becomes useful when it changes the intervention.

Example:

An athlete repeatedly reports that they know the correct technical response but fail to initiate it under pressure.

This suggests the coaching problem may not require more technical information.

FRAME may instead explore:

- recognition speed
- decision confidence
- drilling under resistance
- pressure exposure
- cue simplification

The athlete’s self-awareness helps FRAME choose the correct layer of intervention.

## Calibration
Synochi should gradually learn how reliable different forms of self-report are for the individual athlete.

Some athletes may:

- accurately recognise fatigue
- underestimate emotional disruption
- overestimate technical understanding
- reliably detect when timing deteriorates

The objective is not to judge the athlete.

It is to understand which self-observations predict meaningful outcomes.

## Failure Modes

### Over-Interpretation
FRAME treats one self-report as a stable trait.

Result:
temporary state becomes identity.

### False Certainty
The athlete’s explanation is stored as fact.

Result:
incorrect causal models become reinforced.

### Excessive Introspection
FRAME repeatedly asks the athlete to analyse themselves instead of training.

Result:
self-awareness becomes rumination.

### Generic Reflection
Questions such as:

“How did that make you feel?”

are asked without a clear coaching purpose.

Result:
interaction becomes performative rather than useful.

### Identity Collapse
A temporary failure becomes:

“I am bad at this.”

FRAME should redirect from identity-level conclusions toward observable patterns.

## Coaching Preference
FRAME should use self-awareness to improve observation, not to produce endless introspection.

Prefer:

notice
→ clarify
→ separate observation from explanation
→ identify pattern
→ test through behaviour
→ update model

Every reflective question should earn its place by improving either:

- diagnosis
- intervention
- model accuracy
- athlete agency

If it does none of those, do not ask it.

## Synochi Interpretation
Self-Awareness is an internal sensing layer.

It gives Synochi access to information that external observation alone cannot provide.

But internal sensing is noisy.

The system should therefore combine:

self-report
+ behaviour
+ context
+ outcomes

rather than privileging any one source.

The useful sequence is:

notice internal signal
→ articulate observation
→ separate observation from interpretation
→ compare against external evidence
→ test
→ integrate if useful

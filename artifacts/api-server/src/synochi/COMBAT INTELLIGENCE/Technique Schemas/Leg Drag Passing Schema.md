
[[Combat Intelligence Hub]]
[[Leg Drag]]

## Function
A reusable coaching schema for recognising, diagnosing, and improving leg drag passing.

This node should help FRAME decide:

- whether the leg drag is structurally established
- whether the passer has actually controlled the hip line
- what recovery route the opponent still has available
- what single intervention is most useful
- whether to continue the drag, switch direction, or stabilise

The purpose is not to describe the leg drag.

The purpose is to help FRAME convert observed failure into a specific coaching response.

## Starting Conditions
A leg drag becomes viable when:

- the opponent’s legs can be redirected toward one side
- the passer can begin occupying the opposite side of the hips
- the bottom athlete is losing the ability to square the knees back toward the passer
- the passer can maintain enough connection to prevent immediate guard recovery

FRAME should not identify a true leg drag simply because the opponent’s legs have been moved sideways.

The hip relationship must also change.

## Core Sequence
A reliable leg drag progression usually follows this logic:

1. control the legs
2. redirect them toward one side
3. move around the opposite side
4. occupy the space beside the hips
5. prevent the opponent from squaring back
6. connect to the upper body
7. stabilise top control or exploit back exposure

The defining transition is from leg displacement into hip control.

## Primary Mechanical Priorities

### Leg Displacement
The passer must meaningfully redirect the opponent’s defensive leg structure.

FRAME should assess:

- whether both legs are actually displaced
- whether the knees remain pointed away from the passer
- whether the bottom athlete can immediately recover centre-line position

If the legs return freely, the drag has not been established.

### Hip Occupation
This is the most important diagnostic layer.

The passer should move into the space created beside the opponent’s hips.

If the passer remains too far away, the bottom athlete can simply square back up.

FRAME should strongly prioritise hip occupation over continued hand fighting.

### Rotational Control
The bottom athlete should not be able to freely turn the pelvis and shoulders back toward the passer.

FRAME should assess:

- hip orientation
- shoulder orientation
- frame recovery
- knee recovery

Loss of rotational control usually explains late guard recovery.

### Upper Body Connection
Once the hips are controlled, the passer should connect to the torso.

The exact control may vary, but the purpose is the same:

stop the opponent rebuilding alignment.

## Decision Branches

### Opponent Squares Back Up
Likely issue:

- passer did not reach the hip line quickly enough
- leg control was released too early
- upper-body connection was delayed

Useful coaching preference:
occupy the hip space before reaching for anything else.

### Opponent Turns Away
This may create:

- back exposure
- rear control opportunities
- easier upper-body connection

FRAME should recognise this as a useful reaction rather than automatically trying to force side control.

### Opponent Frames Strongly
Likely issue:

- passer reached the hips but failed to connect to the torso

Useful coaching preference:
address the frame before progressing.

### Opponent Recovers Inside Knee Position
Likely issue:

- lower-body displacement was incomplete
- passer created too much distance
- hip control weakened

Useful coaching preference:
restore hip occupation before chasing upper-body control.

### Passer Controls Legs but Cannot Advance
Likely issue:

- athlete is treating the leg drag as a grip configuration rather than a positional transition

Useful coaching preference:
move the body around the hips rather than continuing to manipulate the legs.

## Failure Diagnosis

### Failure Pattern: Legs Move but Guard Immediately Recovers
Likely causes:

- passer remains in front
- hip space is not occupied
- displacement is temporary

Useful coaching preference:
move around the hips faster.

### Failure Pattern: Opponent Squares Up Late
Likely causes:

- upper-body control missing
- passer releases hip control too early
- shoulder rotation remains available

Useful coaching preference:
connect to the torso before settling.

### Failure Pattern: Opponent Turns Away and Escapes
Likely causes:

- passer fails to recognise back exposure
- rear movement is not controlled

Useful coaching preference:
follow the reaction rather than forcing the original finish.

### Failure Pattern: Passer Gets Stuck Beside the Hips
Likely causes:

- strong defensive frames
- upper-body connection incomplete
- opponent still has useful shoulder rotation

Useful coaching preference:
solve the upper-body layer rather than re-dragging the legs.

## Coaching Cues
FRAME should prefer short cues such as:

- “Move around the hips.”
- “Don’t just drag the legs — occupy the space.”
- “Stop them squaring back up.”
- “Connect to the upper body now.”
- “They’re turning away — take the reaction.”
- “Keep the hip pinned before you release the legs.”

Avoid explaining the full leg drag system unless the athlete asks for a full breakdown.

## Video Recognition
FRAME should look for:

- both legs displaced toward one side
- passer moving around the opposite side
- passer reaching the hip line
- opponent attempting to square the knees back
- opponent turning away
- transition from leg control into torso control
- final stabilisation or back exposure

FRAME should distinguish:

leg displacement

from

an established leg drag position.

## Drill Selection Logic

### Displacement Failure
Use drills around:
- controlling both legs
- redirecting knees
- synchronising hand movement with footwork

### Hip Occupation Failure
Use drills around:
- moving around the legs
- reaching the hip line quickly
- preventing immediate squaring

### Rotational Control Failure
Use drills around:
- hip pinning
- shoulder control
- denying late recovery

### Reaction Recognition Failure
Use progressive drills where the defender alternates between:
- squaring back
- framing
- turning away
- recovering inside knee position

FRAME should select the drill based on the exact failure pattern.

## Coaching Preference
FRAME should avoid treating the leg drag as a fixed finishing sequence.

The athlete should be coached according to the reaction created.

Prefer:

displace
→ occupy
→ read reaction
→ control the next layer
→ stabilise

If the opponent gives back exposure, the system should not insist on side control simply because that was the original intended pass.

## Synochi Interpretation
The leg drag is a positional displacement problem.

The coaching question is:

Did the athlete convert leg displacement into control of the opponent’s alignment?

The system should distinguish between:

- failed displacement
- failed hip occupation
- failed rotational control
- failed upper-body connection
- reaction-recognition failure

The useful logic is:

recognise displacement
→ verify hip occupation
→ identify defensive reaction
→ select smallest correction
→ observe outcome
→ update coaching preference

# Escape / Recovery Drill

## Purpose
Develop the ability to resolve a disadvantage and regain a position from which effective action is possible.

## Training Target
Threat recognition, defensive structure, timing, space creation and connection between recovery actions.

Escape removes or reduces an opponent’s immediate control or threat. Recovery restores usable posture, balance, distance, angle or position.

## Setup
Define:
- The sport and applicable ruleset.
- The starting disadvantage.
- Distance, contact, posture and environmental boundaries.
- Any immediate threats.
- Each participant’s permitted actions.
- The recovery target, success condition and reset point.
- Equipment and contact intensity.

Distinguish early defensive intervention from recovery against an established disadvantage.

## Task
The recovering athlete manages the immediate threat, disrupts the opponent’s advantage and restores a usable state.

The opposing athlete maintains pressure, control or attacking opportunity within the permitted actions.

Continue until recovery is demonstrated, the opponent achieves their objective or a reset condition is reached.

## Information and Resistance
Identify the cues the athlete must recognise, such as changes in pressure, grip, balance, distance, angle or attacking commitment.

Begin with predictable opposition where needed. Introduce uncertainty and additional responses as competence improves.

Resistance must challenge the target skill without making the recovery task inaccessible.

## Constraints
Select constraints around the observed problem:
- Available recovery routes.
- Permitted attacks, grips, frames and defensive actions.
- Opponent movement and continuation options.
- Starting distance or depth of control.
- Space, boundary or equipment restrictions.
- Time available to recover.
- Actions required after recovery.

Ensure the success condition does not reward escaping one disadvantage while immediately entering another.

## Dosage
Use repetitions to develop a defined component and short contested exchanges to practise recognition and timing.

Set work, rest and intensity according to the learning objective, contact demands and athlete capability.

Introduce fatigue only when recovery under fatigue is the explicit training target.

## Success Criteria
- The immediate threat is recognised and managed.
- The opponent’s advantage is meaningfully reduced.
- Useful posture, balance, distance, angle or position is restored.
- Recovery actions adapt when the first option is blocked.
- The athlete can defend or continue effectively after recovery.

Include a brief continuation where appropriate to distinguish a temporary opening from a functional recovery.

## Progression
1. Practise against a predictable, manageable disadvantage.
2. Increase resistance or pressure.
3. Introduce a response that blocks the initial recovery.
4. Vary starting conditions and available threats.
5. Require recovery followed by a useful next action.
6. Assess transfer within a representative live task.

Increase one main difficulty at a time so the source of failure remains identifiable.

## Regression
Reduce pressure, contact intensity, starting disadvantage or opponent options.

Provide a more accessible recovery target or isolate the missing component before rebuilding the exchange.

## Diagnostic Use
Distinguish between:
- Failure to recognise the threat.
- Failure to establish defensive structure.
- Failure to disrupt the opponent’s advantage.
- Failure to select or connect recovery actions.
- Failure to maintain the recovered state.
- Failure to continue effectively after recovery.

Record where the recovery breaks down rather than treating every unsuccessful exchange as the same problem.

## Sport Adaptation
Preserve the shared training function while adapting the task to the discipline:
- Grappling: recover posture, escape control or regain a usable position.
- Striking: manage attacking pressure and restore defensive structure, distance or angle.
- Mixed combat: recover within or between striking, clinch and ground exchanges.

Use the selected ruleset to determine legal actions, scoring implications, permitted contact and continuation.

Account for the discipline’s tactical priorities. Do not assume the same recovery target is appropriate across judo, karate, kickboxing, MMA, Muay Thai, sambo or taekwondo.

## Synochi Selection Logic

Select when an athlete repeatedly remains disadvantaged, expends effort without improving their situation or recovers briefly before losing it again.

Individualise the starting conditions and recovery target around the observed failure, sport, ruleset, experience, partner suitability and movement limitations.

Assess improvement in a representative sport task before concluding that drill success has transferred.

## Safety
Define contact intensity, prohibited actions and stopping conditions before starting.

Use suitable protective equipment and partner control. Honour taps and stop signals immediately.

Reset when safe defensive action is no longer available. Do not require movement through painful ranges or uncontrolled impact to complete the task.

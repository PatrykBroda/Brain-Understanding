# Kneebar

## Type
Leg Lock Submission / Linear Joint Attack

## Purpose
The kneebar is a leg lock that isolates the opponent’s leg and applies extension pressure through the knee.

The visible finish occurs at the knee joint, but the submission depends on controlling the larger relationship between:

- hip
- thigh
- knee line
- lower leg
- rotational freedom

A strong kneebar position prevents the opponent from freely turning, bending the knee, or withdrawing the leg.

## Core Mechanism
The kneebar functions through controlled hyperextension of the knee.

The attacker uses their hips and body position to create a fulcrum while controlling the opponent’s lower leg.

The goal is to align the opponent’s knee with the attacker’s hip line so force transfers efficiently through the joint.

The finishing mechanic becomes weaker when the opponent can rotate the knee, bend the leg, or move the hip freely.

## Structural Characteristics
Typical features include:

- one leg isolated between the attacker’s legs
- the opponent’s knee positioned near the attacker’s hip line
- the thigh controlled strongly enough to prevent easy withdrawal
- the lower leg secured with the arms
- the attacker’s knees controlling the opponent’s thigh
- the opponent’s hip rotation restricted

The attacker should control the leg as a whole rather than pulling only on the foot.

## Positional Priorities
The attacker generally wants to:

- isolate one leg
- control the knee line
- keep the thigh connected tightly
- prevent the opponent from rotating
- control the lower leg close to the body
- maintain hip alignment with the knee
- remove slack before extending

## Rotation Control
Rotational freedom is one of the most important variables in kneebar control.

If the defender can freely turn the knee inward or outward, they may reduce extension pressure and begin escaping.

The attacker should therefore monitor:

- hip orientation
- knee direction
- foot direction
- ability of the defender to spin

A kneebar is strongest when the leg is controlled as one aligned structure.

## Defensive Priorities
The defender commonly attempts to:

- bend the knee
- rotate the hip
- turn the knee away from the finishing line
- clear the attacker’s knees
- withdraw the knee line
- control the attacker’s hands
- connect to the attacker’s legs
- spin or move into a safer alignment

Early rotation is usually easier than late rotation once the thigh is fully controlled.

## Common Failure Modes

### Knee line too shallow
The attacker controls the lower leg but the knee remains outside the strongest part of the hip structure.

Result:
the defender can withdraw the leg.

### Loose thigh control
The opponent’s leg moves independently inside the attacker’s knees.

Result:
rotation and bending become easier.

### Pulling on the foot
The attacker focuses on the ankle rather than controlling the entire leg.

Result:
the defender rotates and relieves pressure.

### Poor hip alignment
The attacker’s hips are not positioned directly against the knee line.

Result:
extension becomes inefficient.

### Overextending before controlling rotation
The attacker attempts to finish while the opponent still has rotational freedom.

Result:
the defender turns out of the pressure.

## Recognition Cues
FRAME may identify a kneebar when:

- one athlete isolates the opponent’s leg between their own legs
- the opponent’s knee is positioned near the attacker’s hips
- the attacker controls the lower leg with both arms
- the defender is trying to bend or rotate the knee
- the exchange revolves around maintaining or extracting the knee line
- the attacker extends the hips while keeping the leg aligned

## Video Analysis
FRAME should assess:

- whether the knee line is deeply controlled
- whether the opponent can bend the leg
- whether the thigh is tightly isolated
- whether the defender can rotate the hip
- whether the attacker’s hips align with the knee
- whether the attacker controls the entire lower leg
- what movement creates the defender’s escape

## Safety Interpretation
Kneebars can damage the knee rapidly if finishing pressure is applied aggressively.

Training application should prioritise:

- progressive extension
- controlled hip pressure
- early tapping
- avoiding explosive bridging
- maintaining awareness during rotational defence

FRAME should treat uncontrolled finishing mechanics as unsafe.

## Coaching Interpretation
If a kneebar repeatedly fails, inspect positional alignment before adding more extension pressure.

Useful questions include:

- Is the knee line deep enough?
- Is the thigh actually trapped?
- Can the opponent rotate?
- Is the attacker controlling the whole leg?
- Are the hips aligned with the knee?

The finish should emerge from positional control rather than force.

## Synochi Interpretation
The kneebar is a linear isolation system.

The attacker removes the opponent’s ability to bend, rotate, or withdraw the leg, then aligns the knee against a controlled extension force.

The useful sequence is:

isolate,
control knee line,
restrict rotation,
align hips,
remove slack,
apply controlled extension.

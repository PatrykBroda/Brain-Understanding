Butterfly guard is a seated or semi-seated guard where the bottom athlete uses one or both insteps as inside hooks against the opponent’s legs while controlling upper-body connection and balance.

Butterfly guard should be understood as an elevation, off-balancing, and connection system rather than simply a guard with hooks inserted.

Primary bottom-player objectives include:

- establish useful upper-body connection
- keep inside position with the legs
- control distance
- prevent the opponent from flattening the hips
- create kuzushi before attempting elevation
- connect upper-body control to lower-body hooks
- force the opponent to post
- expose pathways to sweeps, wrestle-ups, or leg entanglements

Common attacking pathways include:

- butterfly sweep
- shoulder crunch sweep
- arm drag
- wrestle-up
- single-leg entry
- SLX entry
- X-guard entry
- saddle entry
- back exposure
- front headlock transitions

Primary top-player objectives include:

- deny strong inside hooks
- control the bottom athlete’s head and shoulders
- flatten or extend the seated posture
- keep hips outside effective elevation range
- prevent meaningful upper-body attachment
- force the bottom athlete onto a weaker angle
- clear hooks and begin passing

Key mechanical variables include:

- inside leg position
- hip proximity
- upper-body connection
- head position
- angle of the spine
- ability to create elevation
- opponent’s available posts
- direction of off-balance
- connection between hook and upper-body control

Video observation cues:

- hooks present without meaningful upper-body connection
- athlete attempting to elevate before creating kuzushi
- top player successfully flattening seated posture
- opponent forced to post before the sweep
- arm drag creating back exposure
- failed elevation converting into wrestle-up or leg entry
- butterfly hook being used to redirect rather than simply lift

Synochi should recognise butterfly guard as a reaction-driven off-balancing system.

Its effectiveness comes from combining inside position, upper-body connection, and directional kuzushi so that elevation or transition occurs after the opponent’s base has already been compromised.

## **Core Idea**

Escaping is not simply creating movement.

It is identifying **which constraint must be solved first** so that meaningful movement becomes possible.

When multiple controls are present, some are more important than others.

The key question is:

What is currently preventing the escape from beginning?

## **Constraint Order**

A trapped athlete may be dealing with several problems at once:

- head control,
- shoulder control,
- hip control,
- lost inside position,
- collapsed posture,
- lack of base,
- blocked rotation,
- or strong connection.

Trying to solve all of them simultaneously often creates wasted effort.

A better approach is:

**Identify dominant constraint → remove it → rebuild structure → advance escape**

## **Primary Constraint**

The primary constraint is the control that currently prevents the next useful movement.

For example:

If the athlete cannot turn because the head is fixed, recovering hip movement may not be enough.

If the hips are pinned but the upper body is relatively free, solving head position first may have little effect.

Escape priority is therefore context-dependent.

## **Structure Before Movement**

A common mistake is trying to move before rebuilding enough structure to support the movement.

Useful escape preparation may include:

- recovering inside frames,
- reconnecting elbow and knee,
- rebuilding posture,
- recovering head position,
- creating a usable post,
- or shifting onto a stronger base.

The escape often becomes easier once structure has been restored.

## **Inside Position**

Recovering [[Inside Position]] is frequently a high-priority defensive objective.

If the opponent occupies all meaningful inside space, direct movement may be ineffective.

Recovering even one inside frame can:

- create separation,
- protect the torso,
- block reconnection,
- and begin restoring mobility.

## **Breaking Connection**

If the opponent has strong [[Connection]], escape may first require disrupting that connection.

This can mean:

- creating a frame,
- clearing head control,
- separating hips,
- changing angle,
- or inserting a wedge.

Once connection weakens, the bottom athlete gains independent movement.

## **Restoring Base**

Sometimes the athlete has space but cannot use it because they have no meaningful [[Base]].

A post, foot placement, elbow position, or hip angle may need to be restored before a powerful bridge, turn, or recovery becomes possible.

## **Rotation**

Many escapes depend on restoring rotational freedom.

If both shoulders and hips are fixed, rotation may be impossible.

The athlete may first need to free:

- one shoulder,
- one hip,
- the head,
- or an inside frame

before attempting to turn.

## **Escape Sequences**

Escapes often follow an ordered progression.

Example:

**Recover frame → break chest connection → turn onto side → recover knee → rebuild guard**

The final movement works because the earlier constraints were solved first.

Skipping a stage may cause the athlete to fight against the opponent’s strongest control.

## **Defensive Efficiency**

Good escape mechanics reduce unnecessary effort.

Rather than repeatedly bridging or shrimping against a structurally secure pin, the athlete identifies what makes the pin secure and dismantles it.

This creates a more efficient question:

What do I need to recover before the escape becomes mechanically available?

## **Re-Pinning Risk**

An escape is not complete simply because space appears.

If the athlete creates space without rebuilding useful structure, the opponent may reconnect immediately.

Successful escapes should therefore include:

**Break control → create space → rebuild structure → establish new position**

not simply:

**Create space**

## **Video Analysis**

FRAME should look for:

- which constraint is actually preventing movement,
- whether the athlete attacks the correct problem first,
- whether they attempt large movements before rebuilding structure,
- whether inside position is recovered,
- and whether newly created space is converted into a stable defensive position.

A useful observation may be:

You kept trying to shrimp while their crossface still controlled your head. Until that control was cleared, your hips could move but your torso could not rotate with them.

## **Coaching Implication**

Instead of:

“Move more.”

FRAME should identify the priority:

“Clear the head control first, then turn onto your side.”

Or:

“Recover the inside frame before trying to bring the knee through.”

This gives the athlete an ordered solution.

## **Principle**

**Escapes become easier when constraints are solved in the correct order.**

The athlete should not ask only:

How do I get out?

They should ask:

What is stopping the next useful movement, and what must I recover first?

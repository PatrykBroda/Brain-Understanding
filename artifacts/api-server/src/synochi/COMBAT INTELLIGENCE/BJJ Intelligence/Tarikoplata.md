# Tarikoplata

## Type
Shoulder Lock / Upper Body Isolation System

## Purpose
The Tarikoplata is a shoulder lock that uses a leg-assisted control structure to isolate the opponent’s arm and rotate the shoulder into a compromised position.

It commonly appears when the attacker has already established strong upper-body control and can trap the opponent’s arm close to the torso.

The position can function as:

- a direct submission
- a control system
- a transition from Kimura-style attacks
- a way to immobilise the shoulder during top control

## Core Mechanism
The Tarikoplata works by combining arm isolation with lower-body control.

The attacker uses the legs to reinforce control over the opponent’s arm while rotating the shoulder against restricted torso movement.

The effectiveness of the position depends on controlling:

- wrist position
- elbow position
- shoulder rotation
- torso movement
- available escape direction

The submission becomes stronger when the opponent cannot freely turn with the pressure.

## Structural Characteristics
Typical features include:

- one of the opponent’s arms isolated close to the attacker’s body
- the attacker using a leg to reinforce control of the arm
- the opponent’s elbow separated from a safe position
- the wrist and forearm prevented from freely rotating
- the shoulder placed under increasing rotational tension
- the opponent’s torso movement restricted

## Positional Priorities
The attacker generally wants to:

- isolate the arm before attempting the finish
- keep the wrist controlled
- prevent the elbow from reconnecting to the torso
- use the leg to reinforce the upper-body grip
- control the opponent’s shoulder line
- prevent the opponent from turning into the pressure

## Relationship Between Arm and Torso
The Tarikoplata becomes powerful when the opponent’s arm can no longer move independently of the attacking structure.

The attacker uses the trapped limb as a lever while denying the opponent enough torso rotation to relieve pressure.

If the torso is free, the opponent may rotate with the attack and reduce the effect.

## Offensive Pathways
The Tarikoplata may develop from:

- Kimura-style control
- top half guard
- side control
- mount transitions
- scrambles where an arm becomes isolated
- failed shoulder lock attempts

The exact entry can vary, but the finishing structure depends on preserving arm isolation throughout the transition.

## Defensive Priorities
The defender commonly attempts to:

- reconnect the elbow to the torso
- straighten the arm
- rotate the shoulder into safer alignment
- turn the torso with the pressure
- free the trapped wrist
- remove the attacker’s reinforcing leg
- create enough space to restore posture

## Common Failure Modes

### Arm not fully isolated
The attacker attempts the submission while the elbow remains connected to the body.

Result:
the opponent retains strong structural defence.

### Weak wrist control
The opponent can rotate or straighten the forearm.

Result:
the shoulder pressure decreases.

### Leg control too loose
The attacker’s lower-body reinforcement does not meaningfully trap the arm.

Result:
the opponent extracts the limb.

### Torso rotation remains available
The opponent can turn with the submission.

Result:
pressure is dissipated through whole-body movement.

### Chasing the finish too early
The attacker applies rotation before stabilising the position.

Result:
the opponent escapes during the transition.

## Recognition Cues
FRAME may identify a Tarikoplata when:

- one athlete has isolated an opponent’s arm in a Kimura-like structure
- a leg is used to reinforce control of the arm
- the defender’s wrist and elbow are tightly constrained
- the attacker rotates the shoulder while maintaining close body connection
- the defender attempts to straighten the arm or turn with the pressure
- the submission develops from a strong upper-body control position

## Video Analysis
FRAME should assess:

- whether the wrist is genuinely controlled
- whether the elbow is separated from the torso
- whether the reinforcing leg is maintaining control
- whether the defender can rotate the shoulder or torso
- whether the attacker remains connected during the finishing movement
- what exact movement allows the defender to create space

## Coaching Interpretation
If a Tarikoplata repeatedly fails, inspect isolation before rotation.

Useful questions include:

- Is the arm actually trapped?
- Can the elbow reconnect to the torso?
- Is the wrist controlled?
- Is the leg reinforcing the position effectively?
- Can the opponent rotate their torso with the attack?

The finish should emerge from a stable arm-control structure rather than from forcing shoulder rotation.

## Synochi Interpretation
The Tarikoplata is a reinforced shoulder-isolation system.

The attacker combines upper-body control with lower-body reinforcement to reduce the opponent’s ability to reposition the arm or rotate the torso.

The useful sequence is:

isolate arm,
secure wrist,
separate elbow,
reinforce with leg,
control torso,
rotate shoulder,
finish.

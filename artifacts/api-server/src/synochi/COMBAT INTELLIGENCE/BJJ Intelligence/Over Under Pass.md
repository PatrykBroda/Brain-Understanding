## Type
Pressure Passing System / Leg Control Pass

## Purpose
The over under pass is a pressure passing system built around controlling both of the opponent’s legs asymmetrically.

One arm typically controls over one leg while the other arm controls underneath the opposite leg.

This creates a strong connection to the opponent’s hips and allows the passer to compress the guard while progressively moving around the legs.

## Structural Characteristics
Typical features include:

- one arm controlling over a leg
- one arm controlling underneath the opposite leg
- the passer’s chest connected closely to the opponent’s thighs or hips
- head position used to reinforce pressure and deny rotation
- the opponent’s legs compressed together or redirected
- the passer circling toward the side of the overhooked leg

The exact grips vary, but the defining feature is asymmetrical control of the legs combined with heavy hip pressure.

## Core Mechanism
The over under pass works by compressing the opponent’s lower-body structure and reducing independent leg movement.

The passer attempts to:

- bind the legs together
- limit hip rotation
- collapse knee position
- remove space between the opponent’s thighs and torso
- move around the guard while maintaining compression

The pass becomes effective when the opponent can no longer freely separate the knees and reconstruct guard.

## Entry Conditions
Common entries include:

- open guard
- butterfly guard
- half guard transitions
- failed torreando attempts
- reactions to standing passing
- pressure entries from kneeling or crouched positions
- situations where both legs can be collected close together

The position is especially useful when the bottom athlete’s legs become extended or compressed into a narrow defensive structure.

## Head Position
Head position often determines whether the passer can maintain directional pressure.

Useful head placement may:

- block the opponent from turning toward the passer
- prevent strong upper-body frames
- reinforce shoulder pressure
- help pin the hips indirectly

A loose head position can allow the bottom athlete to rotate, frame, or create space.

## Hip Control
The passer should remain closely connected to the opponent’s hip line.

Important goals include:

- preventing the hips from freely turning
- stopping the knees from separating
- keeping the opponent’s legs compressed
- advancing around the legs without creating space

Distance generally weakens the pass.

## Bottom Player Defensive Priorities
The bottom athlete usually wants to:

- separate the knees
- restore hip mobility
- create frames against the passer
- rotate toward the passer
- recover inside leg position
- establish a knee shield or butterfly hook
- free the leg controlled by the underhooking arm
- create enough space to return to open guard

The longer the legs remain compressed, the harder recovery becomes.

## Common Failure Modes

### Loose compression
The passer controls both legs but does not connect the opponent’s knees and hips tightly enough.

Result:
the bottom athlete separates the legs and reconstructs guard.

### Hips too far away
The passer’s arms control the legs but the torso remains distant.

Result:
the opponent retains rotational freedom.

### Head position lost
The bottom athlete can turn toward the passer.

Result:
frames, underhooks, and guard recovery become available.

### Circling without pressure
The passer tries to move around the legs before stabilising the compressed structure.

Result:
the guard follows the passer.

### Overcommitting forward
The passer places too much weight ahead of the base.

Result:
the opponent may create elevation, inversion, or reversal opportunities.

## Recognition Cues
FRAME may identify an over under pass when:

- one arm is visibly over one leg and the other underneath the opposite leg
- the opponent’s knees are compressed together
- the passer is driving chest pressure into the thighs or hips
- the passer circles around the legs rather than attempting to jump over them
- the bottom athlete is trying to separate the knees or recover hip rotation
- the exchange progresses toward [[Side Control]]

## Video Analysis
FRAME should assess:

- whether the opponent’s legs are genuinely compressed
- whether the passer stays connected to the hips
- the direction of the passer’s head
- whether the bottom athlete can turn toward the passer
- whether the knees separate during the pass
- whether the passer loses pressure while circling
- which defensive frame or hip movement creates recovery

## Coaching Interpretation
If an over under pass repeatedly stalls, inspect compression before movement.

The athlete may be trying to circle around a guard that has not actually been constrained.

The pass becomes easier when the opponent’s lower body is first converted into a narrow, connected structure.

## Synochi Interpretation
The over under pass is a compression-based passing system.

The passer reduces the opponent’s lower-body degrees of freedom by binding both legs into a constrained configuration.

Once hip mobility and knee separation are reduced, the passer can advance around the guard with fewer defensive variables to manage.

The strategic sequence is:

collect,
compress,
deny rotation,
circle,
stabilise.

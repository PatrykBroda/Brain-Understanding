# Guillotine

## Type
Front Headlock Submission / Neck Compression Choke

## Purpose
The guillotine is a submission family built around controlling the opponent’s head and neck from a front-facing or angled position.

It commonly appears from:

- front headlock exchanges
- failed takedowns
- wrestling shots
- seated guard
- standing clinches
- scrambles
- snapdowns

The choke is effective when the attacker controls both the opponent’s neck position and their ability to posture or rotate out.

## Core Mechanism
The guillotine works by creating compression around the neck while controlling posture.

Depending on the variation, the finishing mechanics may emphasise:

- arm position under the neck
- wrist orientation
- elbow position
- chest connection
- torso angle
- hip position

The choke becomes significantly stronger when the opponent cannot lift the head or turn into a safer alignment.

## Structural Characteristics
Typical features include:

- the opponent’s head controlled underneath the attacker’s arm
- the attacker’s choking arm positioned around the neck
- the opponent’s posture broken
- close connection between chest, shoulder, and head
- the defender prevented from freely rotating or pulling the head out

The opponent’s arm may be trapped inside or outside the choking configuration depending on the variation.

## Arm In and Arm Out Variations
Guillotines can be broadly divided into arm-in and arm-out structures.

### Arm Out
The opponent’s neck is isolated without their arm inside the choking loop.

This often allows more direct neck compression.

### Arm In
One of the opponent’s arms remains inside the configuration.

This changes:

- available finishing angles
- grip structure
- posture control
- defensive pathways

FRAME should distinguish the configuration when visible.

## Positional Priorities
The attacker generally wants to:

- keep the opponent’s head below their own shoulder line
- prevent posture recovery
- maintain close chest and shoulder connection
- control the opponent’s ability to rotate
- keep the choking structure tight
- avoid creating unnecessary space while adjusting

## Posture Control
Posture is one of the most important variables.

If the opponent can lift the head and straighten the spine, the choke often weakens rapidly.

The attacker should therefore treat posture control as part of the submission rather than as a separate problem.

## Offensive Pathways
The guillotine may lead to:

- direct choke finish
- front headlock control
- sweep
- transition to top position
- back exposure
- transition into another front-headlock attack

A failed guillotine attempt can still produce strong positional advantage if the head remains controlled.

## Defensive Priorities
The defender commonly attempts to:

- restore posture
- create space around the neck
- control the attacker’s choking arm
- rotate toward a safer angle
- move the head toward the open side
- establish strong upper-body connection
- reduce the attacker’s ability to close the elbow and chest around the neck

## Common Failure Modes

### Head position too high
The opponent regains posture.

Result:
the choke loses compression.

### Loose elbow position
The attacker’s choking arm opens away from the body.

Result:
space appears around the neck.

### Pulling with the arms only
The attacker relies on arm strength without controlling posture or torso alignment.

Result:
the defender survives and begins escaping.

### Poor torso angle
The attacker remains badly aligned with the opponent’s neck.

Result:
pressure is distributed inefficiently.

### Sacrificing position for the finish
The attacker falls or rotates without enough control.

Result:
the opponent clears the head and gains top position.

## Recognition Cues
FRAME may identify a guillotine when:

- one athlete controls the opponent’s head underneath an arm
- the defender’s posture is visibly broken
- the attacker connects the hands around the neck
- the defender is trying to lift the head or peel the choking arm
- the exchange originates from a shot, snapdown, or front headlock
- the attacker adjusts torso or elbow position to increase neck compression

## Video Analysis
FRAME should assess:

- whether the opponent can posture
- whether the choking elbow remains tight
- whether the attacker maintains head control
- whether the opponent’s arm is inside or outside
- whether the attacker creates useful torso angle
- whether the defender can rotate toward the open side
- what happens if the choke fails

## Coaching Interpretation
If a guillotine repeatedly fails, inspect posture control before grip strength.

Useful questions include:

- Is the opponent’s head actually controlled?
- Can they straighten the spine?
- Is the choking elbow tight?
- Is the attacker using body position or just pulling?
- Does the attacker retain positional advantage if the choke does not finish?

A strong guillotine should feel structurally restrictive before maximal squeezing is required.

## Synochi Interpretation
The guillotine is a head-control submission system.

The attacker converts control of the opponent’s posture and cervical alignment into a choking opportunity.

The useful sequence is:

capture head,
break posture,
close space,
control rotation,
apply compression,
finish or transition.


## Type
Shoulder Lock / Bent Arm Submission

## Purpose
The Americana is a bent-arm shoulder lock that isolates the opponent’s arm and applies rotational pressure through the shoulder while keeping the elbow controlled.

It commonly appears from dominant top positions where the attacker can pin the opponent’s upper body and separate the arm from the torso.

The submission depends on controlling:

- wrist position
- elbow position
- shoulder rotation
- torso movement
- available escape direction

## Core Mechanism
The attacker bends the opponent’s arm and uses a figure-four grip to control the wrist and elbow.

The shoulder is then rotated while the opponent’s torso is prevented from moving freely with the pressure.

The submission becomes stronger when the elbow is kept close to the mat and the wrist remains controlled.

## Structural Characteristics
Typical features include:

- opponent’s arm bent near ninety degrees
- attacker controlling the wrist
- secondary arm threaded underneath the opponent’s upper arm
- figure-four grip established
- opponent’s elbow pinned or closely controlled
- torso movement restricted

## Positional Priorities
The attacker generally wants to:

- pin the wrist
- prevent the elbow from drifting
- keep the opponent’s shoulder connected to the mat or controlled surface
- maintain tight figure-four structure
- separate the arm from the torso
- restrict whole-body rotation

The submission becomes weaker when the opponent can move the elbow freely or turn the torso with the pressure.

## Wrist and Elbow Relationship
The Americana is not simply a wrist movement.

The wrist and elbow must be controlled together.

If the wrist moves while the elbow remains free, the opponent can change the shoulder angle and reduce pressure.

If the elbow is controlled but the wrist escapes, the submission structure breaks.

The attacker should treat the arm as one connected lever.

## Offensive Pathways
The Americana often appears from:

- mount
- side control
- pressure passing transitions
- upper-body pinning exchanges
- reactions where the opponent frames with an exposed arm

It may also create reactions that open other attacks.

## Defensive Priorities
The defender commonly attempts to:

- reconnect the elbow to the torso
- straighten the arm
- turn onto the side
- move the elbow away from the mat
- control the attacker’s hands
- bridge or rotate the torso
- change the shoulder angle before the figure-four becomes stable

Early elbow recovery is usually easier than escaping once the shoulder is fully isolated.

## Common Failure Modes

### Elbow not controlled
The attacker moves the wrist but allows the elbow to travel.

Result:
the opponent rotates the shoulder and reduces pressure.

### Arm remains too close to the torso
The attacker never creates enough separation.

Result:
the defender retains structural strength.

### Lifting instead of rotating
The attacker pulls the arm upward rather than creating shoulder rotation.

Result:
the finish becomes inefficient.

### Loose figure-four
The attacker’s grip structure is weak.

Result:
the defender straightens or retracts the arm.

### Ignoring torso movement
The opponent turns their whole body with the pressure.

Result:
shoulder tension decreases.

## Recognition Cues
FRAME may identify an Americana when:

- one athlete pins the opponent’s wrist near the mat
- the opponent’s arm is bent
- the attacker threads a second arm beneath the upper arm
- a figure-four grip is established
- the defender tries to straighten or retract the arm
- the attacker rotates the bent arm while controlling the elbow

## Video Analysis
FRAME should assess:

- whether the wrist is secured
- whether the elbow remains controlled
- whether the opponent can rotate the torso
- whether the arm is sufficiently separated from the body
- whether the attacker maintains a compact figure-four
- whether the movement is rotational rather than simply upward
- what defensive movement breaks the structure

## Coaching Interpretation
If the Americana repeatedly fails, inspect elbow control before applying more force.

Useful questions include:

- Is the elbow actually pinned?
- Is the wrist secure?
- Can the opponent turn their torso?
- Is the arm separated enough from the body?
- Is the attacker creating rotation or merely lifting?

The submission should become mechanically tight before significant force is required.

## Synochi Interpretation
The Americana is a bent-arm shoulder-isolation system.

The attacker controls both ends of the lever while preventing the opponent’s torso from rotating freely with the applied pressure.

The useful sequence is:

isolate arm,
secure wrist,
control elbow,
restrict torso movement,
create shoulder rotation,
finish.

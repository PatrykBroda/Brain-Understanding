
## Type
Open Guard Pass / Outside Passing System

## Purpose
The Torreando Pass is an outside passing method built around controlling the opponent’s legs and redirecting them away from the direction the passer intends to move.

Rather than driving directly through the guard, the passer creates an angle around it.

The central objective is to move the opponent’s knees away from the defensive centre line while the passer occupies the space beside the hips.

## Structural Characteristics
Typical features include:

- control of the opponent’s knees, shins, ankles, or lower legs
- the opponent’s legs redirected away from the passer’s movement
- lateral movement around the hip line
- distance maintained long enough to prevent strong upper-body connection from the guard player
- rapid transition from leg control into upper-body control

The pass can be performed from standing, crouched, or transitional open-guard exchanges.

## Core Mechanism
The Torreando Pass works through redirection.

The guard player normally wants their knees and feet positioned between themselves and the passer.

The passer removes that relationship by moving the legs away from the direction of travel.

This creates a temporary opening around the outside of the hips.

The passer must then occupy that opening before the opponent can square back up.

## Positional Priorities
The passer generally wants to:

- control both legs effectively
- move the knees away from the intended passing direction
- maintain posture and mobility
- move laterally rather than directly forward
- reach the opponent’s hip line quickly
- transition into upper-body control before guard recovery

## Directional Movement
The pass depends heavily on timing between hand movement and footwork.

If the hands redirect the legs but the feet remain stationary, the opponent can usually recover.

If the passer moves without sufficiently redirecting the legs, the guard follows them.

The movement should therefore function as one connected action:

redirect,
move,
occupy.

## Bottom Player Defensive Priorities
The guard player commonly attempts to:

- keep the knees facing the passer
- maintain foot or shin connection
- recover inside position
- frame against the passer as they approach
- invert or rotate with the passing direction
- establish grips that slow lateral movement
- recover the knees before chest-to-chest control develops

## Common Failure Modes

### Moving the legs without moving the body
The passer pushes the knees aside but stays directly in front.

Result:
the opponent immediately recovers guard.

### Moving before redirecting
The passer circles while the opponent’s knees remain aligned with them.

Result:
the guard simply tracks the movement.

### Reaching for the upper body too early
The passer releases leg control before reaching a dominant angle.

Result:
the opponent replaces their knees.

### Excessive distance
The passer successfully moves around the legs but remains too far from the hips.

Result:
the opponent has enough time to square back up.

### Driving forward
The passer turns an outside pass into a direct pressure battle.

Result:
the opponent can reconnect frames and defensive leg structure.

## Recognition Cues
FRAME may identify a Torreando Pass when:

- the top athlete controls both of the bottom athlete’s legs
- the legs are rapidly redirected to one side
- the passer moves around the opposite side
- the exchange remains primarily outside the opponent’s knee line
- the bottom athlete repeatedly attempts to square their knees back toward the passer
- the pass ends with the passer occupying the space beside the hips

## Video Analysis
FRAME should assess:

- whether both legs are genuinely controlled
- whether the opponent’s knees are moved away from the passing direction
- whether the passer’s footwork matches the hand movement
- how quickly the passer reaches the hip line
- whether the opponent can square back up
- whether leg control is released too early
- what form of upper-body control follows the pass

## Coaching Interpretation
If Torreando passing repeatedly fails, inspect the relationship between redirection and movement.

The key questions are:

- Did the passer actually move the knees?
- Did their feet move at the same time?
- Did they reach the hip line before releasing control?
- Did the opponent remain able to track them?

The pass is rarely just about pushing the legs harder.

It is about creating and occupying an angle before the guard can reorganise.

## Synochi Interpretation
The Torreando Pass is a directional displacement system.

The passer temporarily moves the opponent’s primary defensive structure away from the route they intend to occupy.

The advantage exists only briefly.

Its effectiveness therefore depends on converting displacement into positional occupation before defensive alignment is restored.

The useful sequence is:

control,
redirect,
move,
occupy,
stabilise.

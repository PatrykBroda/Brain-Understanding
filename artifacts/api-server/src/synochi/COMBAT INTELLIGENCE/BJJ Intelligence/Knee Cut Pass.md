## Type
Guard Pass / Pressure Passing Pathway

## Purpose
The knee cut pass is a high-percentage passing system built around driving one knee across the opponent’s thigh line while controlling the upper body strongly enough to prevent guard recovery.

It is not simply a matter of sliding the knee through.

The pass works when lower-body penetration and upper-body control happen together.

## Structural Characteristics
Typical features include:

- one knee cuts across the opponent’s thigh
- the passer controls or clears the opponent’s inside knee line
- the passer establishes upper-body connection through an underhook, crossface, head position, or equivalent control
- the opponent’s hips are prevented from freely turning back into guard
- the passer progresses toward chest-to-chest control

The exact grips can vary significantly between gi and no-gi, but the structural goal remains similar.

## Core Mechanism
The knee cut separates the opponent’s defensive structures.

The passer attempts to create:

- control above the hips
- penetration below the hips
- directional pressure across the body

This can break the opponent’s ability to keep their knees connected to their upper-body frames.

The pass becomes much stronger when the opponent is forced to defend two problems at once:

upper-body flattening

and

lower-body penetration

## Entry Conditions
Common entries include:

- [[Headquarters Position]]
- open guard passing exchanges
- knee shield half guard
- standing passing transitions
- failed leg-entry attempts
- transitions from body lock or smash passing

The passer does not always need to begin in a textbook headquarters configuration.

What matters is whether the knee line can be crossed without giving the bottom athlete enough space to recover.

## Upper Body Control
Upper-body control is usually the deciding factor.

Useful controls may include:

- underhook
- crossface
- head and arm control
- far shoulder control
- near-side elbow control
- head positioning that blocks the opponent from turning inward

A knee cut with no upper-body control often becomes a temporary leg position rather than a completed pass.

## Lower Body Mechanics
The cutting knee should travel across the thigh line while the trailing leg remains capable of maintaining base and preventing re-guarding.

Important details include:

- controlling the opponent’s knee direction
- preventing the knee shield from rebuilding
- avoiding unnecessary entanglement of the trailing foot
- progressing the hips rather than reaching with the upper body alone

## Bottom Player Defensive Priorities
The bottom athlete commonly attempts to:

- maintain knee shield structure
- recover an underhook
- frame against the passer’s shoulder or neck
- turn onto their side
- insert a butterfly hook
- recover inside knee position
- enter single leg or dogfight pathways
- redirect into [[Half Guard]] or another guard

## Common Failure Modes

### Cutting before controlling the upper body
The knee clears but the opponent can rotate underneath.

Result:
guard recovery or reversal.

### Losing the underhook battle
The bottom athlete secures strong inside upper-body control.

Result:
the passer may be forced into [[Dogfight]] or a wrestling exchange.

### Knee trapped at the hip
The passer penetrates partially but cannot clear the opponent’s thigh structure.

Result:
a stalled half guard position.

### Weight too far forward
The passer reaches with the chest while the hips remain behind.

Result:
the opponent can create space underneath or elevate.

### Weight too far back
The passer controls the leg but does not commit pressure through the torso.

Result:
the opponent rebuilds frames and knee position.

## Recognition Cues
FRAME may identify a knee cut when:

- the top athlete’s knee travels across the opponent’s thigh
- the opponent is defending with knee shield or half guard structures
- the passer is trying to establish chest-to-chest connection
- the top athlete is fighting for an underhook or crossface
- the exchange repeatedly revolves around clearing the trapped lower leg
- successful completion leads toward [[Side Control]]

## Video Analysis
FRAME should assess:

- who controls the underhook
- whether the bottom athlete remains on their side
- whether the passer’s head position blocks rotation
- whether the cutting knee has actually cleared the thigh
- whether the trailing leg is trapped
- whether the passer is controlling the hips or simply moving forward
- what recovery route the bottom athlete is attempting

## Coaching Interpretation
If a knee cut repeatedly stalls, the problem may not be the knee.

Check:

- upper-body control
- head position
- hip direction
- underhook ownership
- trailing leg freedom
- the opponent’s ability to turn toward the passer

This prevents technical diagnosis from becoming overly focused on the visible cutting leg.

## Synochi Interpretation
The knee cut is a coordinated constraint problem.

The passer is attempting to deny rotation above the hips while crossing the defensive leg structure below them.

If either layer remains free, the opponent can reconnect their guard.

A successful knee cut therefore represents simultaneous control of:

orientation,
hip access,
and defensive reconstruction.

# Toehold

## Type
Leg Lock Submission / Rotational Foot and Ankle Attack

## Purpose
The toehold is a rotational leg lock that uses control of the foot to create torque through the ankle and lower leg.

Although the grip is applied to the foot, the effectiveness of the submission depends on controlling the larger leg structure strongly enough that the opponent cannot simply rotate, straighten, or withdraw.

## Core Mechanism
The attacker controls the opponent’s foot with a figure-four style grip and rotates the foot while restricting movement through the knee and hip.

The submission works by creating torsion through:

- foot
- ankle
- lower leg
- knee orientation

The more rotational freedom the opponent retains, the easier it is for them to relieve pressure.

## Structural Characteristics
Typical features include:

- control around the toes or forefoot
- secondary control around the opponent’s ankle or lower leg
- a figure-four grip
- the opponent’s knee line at least partially controlled
- the attacker positioned so the opponent cannot freely spin

The exact finishing angle may vary depending on the leg entanglement and orientation.

## Positional Priorities
The attacker generally wants to:

- control the foot securely
- keep the opponent’s knee from freely rotating
- reduce space between their own body and the trapped leg
- maintain a stable figure-four connection
- control the opponent’s escape direction
- apply rotation progressively rather than explosively

## Rotational Control
The defender’s ability to rotate is a major variable.

If the opponent can turn their hip, knee, and foot together, the pressure may dissipate.

The attacker therefore benefits from controlling:

- knee orientation
- hip movement
- lower-leg alignment
- space around the trapped leg

The submission becomes stronger when rotation is localised rather than shared across the whole body.

## Defensive Priorities
The defender commonly attempts to:

- hide or retract the foot
- straighten the leg
- rotate with the pressure
- free the knee line
- break the attacker’s grip
- use the free leg to create space
- change the angle before the lock becomes tight

The safest escape direction depends on the exact orientation of the lock.

## Common Failure Modes

### Foot controlled without leg control
The attacker secures the grip but the opponent’s knee and hip remain free.

Result:
the opponent rotates out of pressure.

### Grip too shallow
The attacker controls only the toes without stable support around the ankle.

Result:
the grip slips or produces weak leverage.

### Excessive arm strength
The attacker tries to finish by twisting harder with the arms.

Result:
control deteriorates and the opponent gains space.

### Poor body position
The grip is mechanically correct but the attacker’s torso and hips are too far away.

Result:
the opponent can move the leg freely.

### Ignoring escape direction
The attacker applies pressure without controlling where the opponent can rotate.

Result:
the defender follows the motion and escapes.

## Recognition Cues
FRAME may identify a toehold when:

- one athlete controls the opponent’s foot with both arms
- a figure-four grip forms around the foot and ankle
- the opponent’s foot is rotated while the leg remains trapped
- the defender attempts to spin or straighten the leg
- the exchange centres around control of the foot and knee orientation

## Video Analysis
FRAME should assess:

- whether the grip is secure
- how much rotational freedom the defender has
- whether the knee line is controlled
- whether the attacker stays close to the trapped leg
- whether the free leg is affecting the exchange
- what exact movement allows the defender to escape

## Safety Interpretation
Toeholds can create fast pressure through the ankle and knee.

Training application should prioritise:

- controlled rotational pressure
- early tapping
- avoiding explosive finishing
- avoiding uncontrolled spinning escapes
- awareness of the ruleset being used

FRAME should avoid encouraging forceful rotational finishing.

## Coaching Interpretation
If a toehold repeatedly fails, inspect control before rotation.

Useful questions include:

- Is the foot actually secured?
- Can the opponent rotate their knee freely?
- Is the attacker close enough to the leg?
- Is the grip stable?
- Is the opponent escaping by moving the entire limb with the pressure?

The finish becomes much more reliable when the leg is controlled as a complete structure.

## Synochi Interpretation
The toehold is a rotational distal-control system.

The attacker uses the foot as a lever while attempting to prevent the rest of the leg from safely following the applied rotation.

The useful sequence is:

secure foot,
control leg,
restrict rotation,
establish angle,
apply controlled torque.

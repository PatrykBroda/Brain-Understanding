
[[Supine Open Guard]]
[[Combat Intelligence Hub]]

Spider Guard is an open guard configuration where the bottom athlete uses foot placement against the opponent’s arms, usually around the biceps or upper arms, together with sleeve or wrist control to manage distance, disrupt posture, and create off balance.

It should be understood as a limb control and distance management system rather than simply a flexible open guard.

Primary bottom player objectives include:

- control the opponent’s arms
- maintain tension through the legs and grips
- prevent the opponent from freely closing distance
- disrupt posture and shoulder alignment
- create asymmetry between the opponent’s left and right sides
- force predictable stepping or posting reactions
- transition into sweeps, triangles, omoplatas, or other open guards

Common attacking pathways include:

- tripod style sweep
- sickle style sweep
- triangle
- omoplata
- armbar
- lasso transitions
- De La Riva Guard
- Single Leg X Guard
- technical stand up
- back exposure from overreaction

Primary top player objectives include:

- remove or neutralise foot control
- break sleeve or wrist control
- keep elbows from being extended away from the body
- recover posture
- control the bottom athlete’s legs
- create an angle around the guard
- avoid stepping into predictable off balancing patterns

Key mechanical variables include:

- foot placement
- grip integrity
- leg tension
- shoulder control
- elbow extension
- hip angle
- opponent stance
- ability to create asymmetric pressure

Video observation cues:

- feet placed on the arms without meaningful tension
- opponent freely retracting the elbows
- bottom athlete creating strong push pull asymmetry
- posture broken before a sweep or submission develops
- top player successfully controlling the feet before passing
- transition from Spider Guard into Lasso Guard or another open guard
- bottom athlete overextending the legs and losing connection

Synochi should recognise Spider Guard as an upper limb control system that uses the legs to disrupt posture, manage distance, and create asymmetric reactions.

Its effectiveness comes from combining grip control with active leg tension and angle rather than relying on foot placement alone.

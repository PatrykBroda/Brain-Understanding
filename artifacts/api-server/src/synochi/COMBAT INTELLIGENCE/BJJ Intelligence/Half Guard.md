Half guard is a position where the bottom athlete controls one of the top athlete’s legs while both players contest upper-body connection, hip position, and the ability to turn or build height.

Half guard should be understood as a family of positions rather than a single guard.

Common configurations include:

- knee shield
- flattened half guard
- underhook half guard
- lockdown
- half butterfly
- seated half guard
- deep half guard
- reverse half guard

Primary bottom-player objectives include:

- avoid being flattened
- win useful head and arm position
- preserve knee-elbow connection
- create an underhook or inside frame
- maintain access to hip rotation
- build to an elbow or hand
- recover full guard, wrestle up, or enter attacking pathways

Primary top-player objectives include:

- flatten the bottom athlete
- control head direction
- win the underhook battle
- separate knees and elbows
- immobilise the trapped leg configuration
- clear the knee line
- progress to mount, side control, or passing sequences

Common bottom pathways include:

- dogfight
- wrestle-up
- knee shield attacks
- butterfly elevation
- back exposure from underhook battles
- deep half transitions
- guard recovery

Common top pathways include:

- crossface and underhook control
- half guard smash
- knee cut progression
- backstep
- tripod passing
- mount
- side control

Key mechanical variables include:

- head position
- underhook control
- knee shield integrity
- hip angle
- trapped-leg depth
- ability to build height
- shoulder alignment
- top-player chest connection

Video observation cues:

- bottom athlete flattened with both shoulders controlled
- top player winning head and underhook position
- knee shield collapsing
- bottom athlete successfully building onto elbow or hand
- underhook creating access to dogfight
- trapped knee becoming free
- top player changing angle rather than forcing directly through frames

Synochi should recognise half guard as a dynamic battle over upper-body alignment, hip freedom, and height.

The position changes dramatically depending on whether the bottom athlete remains mobile and angled or becomes flattened and structurally compressed.

## Type
Guard Passing Position / Transitional Control System

## Purpose
The leg drag is a passing position built around redirecting both of the opponent’s legs to one side while the passer moves around the opposite side of the hips.

Its value comes from creating hip misalignment.

The bottom athlete’s legs are displaced away from the side they need to defend, while the passer begins occupying the space beside the hips.

## Structural Characteristics
Typical features include:

- both of the bottom athlete’s legs are directed toward one side
- the passer controls near the knees, shins, ankles, or hip line
- the passer positions their body beside the opponent’s hips
- the bottom athlete is prevented from squaring their knees back toward the passer
- the passer begins connecting upper-body control to the displaced lower-body structure

The leg drag can function as a passing position, a transitional control state, or an entry into back exposure.

## Core Mechanism
The leg drag works through rotational mismatch.

The opponent’s lower body is turned away while the passer advances around the hips.

This creates a structural problem:

the legs are pointing one direction

while the opponent needs to rotate the torso and hips back toward the passer to recover guard.

The passer’s objective is to prevent that reconnection.

## Entry Conditions
Common entries include:

- [[Headquarters Position]]
- torreando passing exchanges
- failed guard retention
- De La Riva passing
- reverse De La Riva passing
- transitions from knee cut reactions
- redirects from seated or supine open guard
- defensive reactions to leg-entry attempts

The leg drag often appears when the passer redirects rather than drives directly through the guard.

## Hip Control
Control of the hip line is critical.

The passer may use:

- knee pressure
- shin control
- hip posting
- torso connection
- near-side underhook
- waist control
- head position

The goal is to stop the bottom athlete from simply rotating back to face the passer.

## Upper Body Progression
Once the legs are displaced, the passer should connect to the upper body.

Common progressions include:

- chest-to-chest control
- near-side underhook
- crossface
- far shoulder control
- seatbelt-style connection if the back becomes exposed
- transition to [[Side Control]]
- transition to [[Back Control]]

Without upper-body connection, the leg drag may remain temporary.

## Bottom Player Defensive Priorities
The bottom athlete generally wants to:

- square the hips back toward the passer
- recover knee elbow connection
- frame against the passer’s torso
- create shoulder rotation
- insert the near knee back inside
- free the dragged legs before upper-body control settles
- turn strategically rather than remaining twisted

Early recovery is usually easier than late recovery.

## Common Failure Modes

### Controlling legs without controlling hips
The passer moves the legs aside but does not occupy the space beside the hips.

Result:
the opponent simply squares back up.

### Staying too far away
The passer creates displacement but leaves too much distance.

Result:
the bottom athlete rebuilds open guard.

### Reaching for upper-body control too early
The passer abandons lower-body control before the hips are stabilised.

Result:
the legs return to the centre line.

### Allowing shoulder rotation
The bottom athlete turns strongly toward the passer.

Result:
guard recovery becomes available.

### Driving straight forward
The passer treats the leg drag like a pressure pass rather than a redirection.

Result:
the opponent regains alignment and defensive structure.

## Recognition Cues
FRAME may identify a leg drag when:

- both bottom legs are displaced toward the same side
- the passer is positioned beside rather than directly in front of the hips
- the bottom athlete is visibly twisted or attempting to square back
- the passer controls the knees, shins, ankles, or hip line
- the exchange progresses toward side control or back exposure
- guard recovery depends on rotating the hips back toward the passer

## Video Analysis
FRAME should assess:

- whether both legs are genuinely displaced
- whether the passer has reached the side of the hips
- whether the bottom athlete can square back
- how the passer controls the near hip
- whether upper-body connection is established
- whether the bottom athlete turns toward or away
- whether the leg drag becomes [[Side Control]] or [[Back Control]]
- at what moment the bottom athlete loses alignment

## Coaching Interpretation
If a leg drag repeatedly fails, inspect whether the athlete is actually controlling the hip line.

Moving the legs is only the visible entry.

The pass becomes effective when the passer occupies the displaced side and prevents the opponent from rebuilding alignment.

## Synochi Interpretation
The leg drag is an alignment-disruption passing system.

The passer separates the orientation of the opponent’s lower body from the direction needed for effective guard retention.

The resulting advantage comes from forcing the bottom athlete to spend time restoring alignment while the passer advances into control.

The critical transition is:

displace,
occupy,
connect,
stabilise.

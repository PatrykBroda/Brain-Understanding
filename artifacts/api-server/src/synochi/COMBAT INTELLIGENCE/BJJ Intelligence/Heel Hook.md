# Heel Hook

## Type
Leg Lock Submission / Rotational Joint Attack

## Purpose
The heel hook is a leg lock that applies rotational force through the foot and lower leg while controlling the opponent’s knee line and hip movement.

The visible grip is on the heel, but the submission depends on controlling the larger leg structure.

A strong heel hook position typically combines:

- heel exposure
- knee line control
- hip connection
- rotational control
- restricted escape direction

## Core Mechanism
The heel acts as a rotational handle on the leg.

When the foot is controlled and the opponent’s knee cannot freely rotate or withdraw, rotation at the foot transfers force through the ankle and knee.

The attack therefore depends on two linked problems:

control the leg

and

apply rotational finishing mechanics.

Without positional control, the opponent may rotate, slip the knee line, or disengage.

## Inside and Outside Heel Hooks
Heel hooks are commonly divided into inside and outside configurations.

### Inside Heel Hook
The heel is exposed toward the inside of the attacker’s body.

This configuration often creates strong rotational control and may produce significant pressure through the knee.

### Outside Heel Hook
The heel is exposed toward the outside.

The finishing direction and surrounding entanglement differ, but the same broad principles remain:

control the knee line,
limit rotation,
expose the heel,
apply controlled rotational pressure.

FRAME should distinguish the finishing orientation where footage or user description makes it clear.

## Positional Priorities
Before attacking, the controlling athlete generally wants to:

- secure the opponent’s knee line
- keep the hips connected to the trapped leg
- prevent free defensive rotation
- control the opponent’s secondary leg when necessary
- expose the heel without sacrificing the entanglement
- maintain a stable finishing angle

The submission becomes less reliable when heel exposure is prioritised over positional control.

## Heel Exposure
Heel exposure is the process of creating access to the back or side of the opponent’s heel.

This may occur through:

- rotational movement
- controlling the toes
- manipulating the opponent’s knee orientation
- creating reactions with alternative attacks
- adjusting the entanglement

The goal is to expose the heel while keeping the knee trapped.

## Defensive Priorities
The defender generally wants to:

- hide the heel
- extract the knee line
- create rotational freedom
- separate the attacker’s legs
- control the attacker’s hands
- disengage the hips
- move in the correct direction for the specific entanglement

Rotating blindly can increase danger.

Defensive movement must match the entanglement and finishing direction.

## Common Failure Modes

### Chasing the heel before securing the knee
The attacker gains heel exposure but the opponent’s knee remains free.

Result:
the opponent withdraws the leg.

### Loose hip connection
The attacker’s hips drift away from the trapped leg.

Result:
rotational control decreases.

### Weak knee line control
The opponent’s knee clears the controlling structure.

Result:
the heel hook loses most of its finishing potential.

### Over-rotating the upper body
The attacker attempts to generate force through the arms and torso rather than maintaining positional alignment.

Result:
control may weaken and the opponent can rotate with the attack.

### Ignoring the secondary leg
The defender uses the free leg to clear grips, push away, or create escape space.

Result:
the entanglement deteriorates.

## Recognition Cues
FRAME may identify a heel hook when:

- one athlete isolates the opponent’s leg
- the heel is being controlled or exposed
- the attacker’s forearm wraps around the heel line
- the defender is attempting to hide the heel or rotate
- the exchange centres around knee line control
- the attacker maintains a leg entanglement while applying rotational pressure

## Video Analysis
FRAME should assess:

- whether the knee line is controlled
- whether the heel is genuinely exposed
- whether the attacker’s hips remain connected
- whether the defender has rotational freedom
- whether the secondary leg is influencing the exchange
- whether the attacker maintains positional control while entering the finish
- which heel-hook orientation is being used

## Safety Interpretation
Heel hooks can create serious injury before pain becomes a reliable warning signal.

Training application should therefore prioritise:

- controlled finishing pressure
- early tapping
- partner awareness
- ruleset awareness
- avoiding uncontrolled rotational escapes

FRAME should avoid encouraging explosive finishing mechanics.

## Coaching Interpretation
If a heel hook repeatedly fails, inspect the entanglement before the grip.

Useful questions include:

- Is the knee line actually trapped?
- Can the opponent rotate freely?
- Is the attacker connected at the hips?
- Is the heel being exposed without losing control?
- Is the defender’s secondary leg creating the escape?

Many finishing failures are positional failures first.

## Synochi Interpretation
The heel hook is a rotational isolation system.

The attacker controls the opponent’s leg strongly enough that rotation applied at the heel cannot be safely dissipated through movement of the hip or knee.

The useful sequence is:

isolate,
control knee line,
restrict rotation,
expose heel,
secure grip,
apply controlled finish.

Butterfly Ashi is a leg-entanglement configuration where the attacker controls one of the opponent’s legs while using a butterfly-style inside hook to control the far hip or thigh and influence the opponent’s ability to rotate, stand, or withdraw.

It should be understood as a control, off-balancing, and transitional leg-entanglement position rather than simply a variation of Single-Leg X.

Primary attacking objectives include:

- control the opponent’s knee line
- maintain strong hip-to-leg connection
- use the butterfly hook to influence the opponent’s far hip
- restrict effective rotation
- disrupt the opponent’s base
- control distance between the hips
- expose the primary leg while limiting support from the secondary leg
- create predictable reactions that lead into finishing or positional transitions

The butterfly hook provides additional control over the opponent’s overall lower-body structure.

Rather than controlling only the isolated leg, the attacker can use the far-side connection to influence how the opponent’s hips rotate and where their weight can move.

Common attacking pathways include:

- straight ankle lock
- Achilles lock
- heel exposure where ruleset permits
- sweep to top position
- transition to Single-Leg X
- transition to X Guard
- transition to Saddle
- transition to Cross Ashi
- secondary-leg attacks
- back exposure when the opponent rotates defensively

Primary defensive objectives include:

- recover the controlled knee line
- remove or neutralise the butterfly hook
- regain freedom of hip rotation
- separate the attacker’s knees and hip connection
- recover the secondary leg as a usable post
- rebuild base before attempting full disengagement

Key mechanical variables include:

- knee-line depth
- butterfly-hook placement
- far-hip control
- hip connection
- orientation of the controlled knee
- secondary-leg availability
- rotational freedom
- distance between the athletes’ hips

Video observation cues:

- attacker controlling the primary leg while the butterfly hook disrupts the far hip
- opponent unable to freely rotate because both sides of the lower body are being influenced
- butterfly hook being lost and the position degrading toward weaker Single-Leg X control
- defensive weight shift creating elevation or sweeping opportunity
- attacker chasing the foot while losing control of the knee line
- opponent clearing the hook before beginning leg extraction
- transition into Saddle or another ashi configuration as the opponent rotates

Synochi should recognise Butterfly Ashi as a two-sided lower-body control configuration.

Its value comes from combining primary-leg isolation with influence over the opponent’s opposite hip and base, allowing off-balancing, rotational control, and leg-attack transitions to operate together.

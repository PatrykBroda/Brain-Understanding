
## Type
Pressure Passing System / Leg Elevation Pass

## Purpose
The Double Under Pass is a pressure passing system built around controlling underneath both of the opponent’s legs, elevating the hips, and progressively folding the opponent’s lower body toward the upper body.

The passer removes the opponent’s ability to freely use the legs as defensive frames and converts the guard into a compressed structure.

## Structural Characteristics
Typical features include:

- both of the passer’s arms positioned underneath the opponent’s legs
- control around the thighs, hips, or lower back
- the opponent’s hips elevated from the mat
- the knees driven toward the torso
- the passer maintaining close pressure while moving toward one side
- the opponent’s ability to rotate or reinsert the legs progressively reduced

The position often develops from open guard when the passer gains access underneath both legs.

## Core Mechanism
The Double Under Pass works by folding the opponent around their own hips.

Normally, the guard player needs hip mobility and leg extension to maintain distance and recover position.

The passer takes away both.

By lifting the hips and compressing the knees toward the torso, the passer reduces:

- leg mobility
- hip rotation
- ability to frame with the feet
- ability to establish hooks
- ability to square back toward the passer

The passer then moves around the compressed structure.

## Positional Priorities
The passer generally wants to:

- secure control underneath both legs
- elevate the hips
- prevent the opponent from extending the legs
- keep close torso connection
- control the direction of the opponent’s hips
- progressively move around the guard
- establish upper-body control before releasing the legs

## Stack Pressure
Stacking commonly appears within the pass.

The goal is not simply to drive the opponent’s knees toward their face.

Effective stack pressure should reduce the opponent’s usable hip movement while helping the passer control direction.

Poor stacking can create unnecessary force without improving the position.

Useful stack pressure should create:

compression,
hip elevation,
and reduced rotational freedom.

## Directional Progression
Once the opponent’s hips are elevated and compressed, the passer usually begins moving toward one side.

The passer should maintain enough control that the opponent cannot simply follow the movement with the hips.

The progression should feel like moving around a controlled structure rather than racing around free legs.

## Bottom Player Defensive Priorities
The guard player commonly attempts to:

- prevent both arms entering underneath the legs
- widen the knees
- create frames against the passer’s shoulders or head
- lower the hips back toward the mat
- establish hip rotation
- pummel a leg back inside
- invert or redirect before the compression becomes stable
- create enough distance to rebuild open guard

The position becomes significantly harder to escape once the hips are fully elevated and the knees are compressed.

## Common Failure Modes

### Hips remain on the mat
The passer controls underneath the legs but never meaningfully elevates the pelvis.

Result:
the opponent retains hip movement and can recover guard.

### Arms too shallow
The passer’s control remains near the knees rather than connecting more deeply to the hips.

Result:
the opponent can extend the legs and create distance.

### Excessive forward pressure
The passer focuses on stacking without controlling direction.

Result:
the opponent may rotate, invert, or redirect the pass.

### Moving around too early
The passer begins circling before the hips are controlled.

Result:
the guard follows the movement.

### Releasing the legs before upper-body control
The passer clears the lower body but creates a moment of separation.

Result:
the opponent recovers guard before the pass stabilises.

## Recognition Cues
FRAME may identify a Double Under Pass when:

- both of the passer’s arms are underneath the opponent’s legs
- the opponent’s hips are visibly elevated
- both knees are compressed toward the torso
- the bottom athlete is being stacked
- the passer progressively moves around one side
- the defender is trying to widen the knees or lower the hips
- the pass completes after the legs have been compressed and cleared

## Video Analysis
FRAME should assess:

- whether both underhooks are secure
- whether the hips are actually elevated
- how deeply the passer controls the legs
- whether the opponent can rotate the pelvis
- whether stack pressure is creating meaningful constraint
- when the passer begins moving around the guard
- whether upper-body control is established before leg control is released

## Coaching Interpretation
If the Double Under Pass repeatedly stalls, inspect whether the athlete has actually controlled the hips.

The visible problem may appear to be difficulty moving around the legs.

The deeper problem is often that the opponent’s pelvis remains free.

Useful questions include:

- Are the hips elevated?
- Can the opponent extend their legs?
- Can the pelvis rotate?
- Is the passer connected deeply enough?
- Is the passer moving around a controlled structure or chasing moving legs?

## Synochi Interpretation
The Double Under Pass is a compression-and-elevation passing system.

The passer reduces the opponent’s lower-body freedom by lifting the pelvis and folding the legs toward the torso.

This converts a mobile guard into a constrained structure that can be navigated around.

The useful sequence is:

enter underneath,
elevate,
compress,
control direction,
circle,
stabilise.

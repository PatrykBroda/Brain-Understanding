# Omoplata

## Type
Shoulder Lock / Guard Control and Transition System

## Purpose
The Omoplata is a shoulder-control system built around trapping one of the opponent’s arms between the attacker’s legs while using the hips and torso to rotate the opponent’s shoulder away from a safe alignment.

Although commonly treated as a submission, it also functions as:

- a sweep
- a posture-breaking system
- a control position
- a transition to top position
- a route into further shoulder attacks

The position becomes strong when the attacker controls both the isolated shoulder and the opponent’s ability to rotate or posture out.

## Core Mechanism
The Omoplata works by isolating the opponent’s arm and using the attacker’s legs and hips to rotate the shoulder internally while preventing the opponent from turning with the pressure.

The submission therefore depends on controlling:

- arm isolation
- shoulder orientation
- posture
- hip distance
- rotational escape

The leg structure traps the arm, but the attacker’s body position determines whether that isolation becomes meaningful control.

## Structural Characteristics
Typical features include:

- one of the opponent’s arms trapped between the attacker’s legs
- the attacker’s thigh controlling across the opponent’s shoulder
- the opponent’s posture broken forward or sideways
- the attacker positioned at an angle beside the opponent
- the opponent prevented from freely rolling or turning
- the attacker progressing toward sitting up over the trapped shoulder

## Positional Priorities
The attacker generally wants to:

- keep the opponent’s arm trapped
- control the opponent’s hip or waist
- prevent forward rolling
- prevent posture recovery
- rotate their own body toward the opponent’s side
- move toward an upright finishing position
- keep the opponent’s shoulder internally rotated

## Angle Creation
The Omoplata becomes much stronger when the attacker moves off the opponent’s centre line.

Remaining directly underneath or behind the opponent often leaves too much rotational freedom.

Creating angle helps:

- increase shoulder isolation
- prevent posture recovery
- improve hip control
- make rolling escapes harder
- create a cleaner finishing line

## Control of the Opponent’s Hips
Hip control is a major part of finishing and stabilising the position.

If the opponent can freely move the hips, they may:

- roll forward
- spin
- posture
- rotate the shoulder into a safer alignment

The attacker may use the arms, legs, or torso position to prevent this movement.

## Offensive Pathways
The Omoplata may lead to:

- shoulder lock finish
- sweep to top position
- transition into another shoulder attack
- back exposure
- upper-body control after the opponent rolls
- re-entry into guard attacks if the position breaks down

A failed submission attempt can still produce strong positional advantage.

## Defensive Priorities
The defender commonly attempts to:

- posture before the attacker sits up
- roll forward
- rotate the trapped shoulder
- free the elbow
- control the attacker’s legs
- move the hips away from the finishing angle
- prevent the attacker from controlling the waist

Early posture recovery is usually easier than escaping after the attacker has sat upright and controlled the hips.

## Common Failure Modes

### Remaining flat
The attacker isolates the shoulder but stays on the back.

Result:
the opponent retains more rotational freedom.

### Failing to control the hips
The attacker focuses entirely on the trapped arm.

Result:
the opponent rolls or spins out.

### Sitting up without angle
The attacker comes upright directly behind the opponent.

Result:
shoulder pressure is reduced.

### Loose leg control
The opponent’s elbow slips free from the attacking structure.

Result:
the Omoplata collapses.

### Chasing the shoulder lock too early
The attacker applies rotational pressure before stabilising posture and hip control.

Result:
the opponent uses the movement to escape.

## Recognition Cues
FRAME may identify an Omoplata when:

- one athlete traps an opponent’s arm between the legs
- the attacker’s thigh sits across the opponent’s shoulder
- the opponent is folded forward or sideways
- the attacker begins rotating toward an upright seated position
- the defender attempts to roll forward
- the exchange revolves around shoulder rotation and hip control

## Video Analysis
FRAME should assess:

- whether the arm remains fully isolated
- whether the attacker creates angle
- whether the opponent can posture
- whether the attacker controls the hips
- whether the defender can roll freely
- whether the attacker reaches an upright controlling position
- whether the attack becomes a submission, sweep, or transition

## Coaching Interpretation
If an Omoplata repeatedly fails, inspect control of the opponent’s posture and hips before adding more shoulder pressure.

Useful questions include:

- Is the arm genuinely trapped?
- Can the opponent roll freely?
- Has the attacker created enough angle?
- Is the attacker sitting up into control?
- Can the opponent posture before the position stabilises?

The Omoplata becomes reliable when shoulder isolation is combined with control of the opponent’s larger body movement.

## Synochi Interpretation
The Omoplata is a shoulder-isolation and rotational-control system.

The attacker uses the legs to isolate the arm, then progressively restricts the opponent’s ability to posture, rotate, or roll safely.

The useful sequence is:

isolate arm,
break posture,
create angle,
control hips,
sit up,
restrict rotation,
finish or transition.

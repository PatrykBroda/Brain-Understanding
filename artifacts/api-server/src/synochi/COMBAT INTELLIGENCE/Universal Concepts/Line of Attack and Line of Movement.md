## Type
Universal Concept

## Domain
[[Combat Intelligence Hub]]

## Definition
Line of Attack and Line of Movement describe the path along which an athlete directs force, body mass, limbs, or positional intent through space.

The line of attack is the path through which an offensive action is projected.

The line of movement is the path along which the athlete or opponent is actually travelling.

These lines may align, diverge, intersect, or change during the exchange.

## Core Principle
Combat geometry changes when movement and force travel along different lines.

**Position → Line → Direction → Intersection → Mechanical consequence**

A successful action often depends on creating a line the opponent is poorly organised to defend.

## Why It Matters
Many techniques fail not because the athlete chose the wrong action, but because they attacked along a strong defensive line.

Examples:

- shooting directly into square hips
- striking down the centre against a well-covered guard
- passing straight into frames
- pulling against a stable base
- retreating along the same line as incoming pressure

Changing the line can change the entire mechanical problem.

## Line of Attack
The line of attack is the direction along which offensive force or threat is projected.

Examples:

- straight-line strike
- diagonal knee cut
- rotational arm drag
- level change into hips
- snapdown through head-and-spine line

The same target can often be attacked through several different lines.

## Line of Movement
The line of movement is the actual path taken by the athlete’s body or base.

Possible lines include:

- forward
- backward
- lateral
- diagonal
- circular
- rotational

Movement direction influences which attacks and defensive actions remain available.

## Alignment Between Lines
The athlete’s line of movement and line of attack may align.

Example:

Step forward  
→ hips follow  
→ strike travels forward

This can maximise force transfer.

They may also deliberately differ.

Example:

Step laterally  
→ attack travels diagonally inward

This creates angle and asymmetry.

## Intersecting Lines
Many successful exchanges occur when one athlete moves off the opponent’s primary line while maintaining their own offensive line.

Example:

Opponent drives forward  
→ athlete pivots outside  
→ incoming line misses centre  
→ counterattack enters from new angle

The key advantage comes from changing the relationship between the two lines.

## Strong and Weak Lines
Athletes tend to have directions in which their structure is stronger or weaker.

A strong line may be supported by:

- aligned base
- hips behind force
- stable posture
- available posts

A weak line may contain:

- poor support
- restricted rotation
- narrow base
- overloaded limb
- limited recovery options

Synochi should therefore evaluate line quality relative to structure.

## Line Change
Lines can change mid-action.

Example:

Initial forward entry  
→ opponent resists  
→ athlete pivots  
→ force redirects laterally

Changing line can transform a stalled attack into a successful one.

## Cutting the Line
An athlete can intercept an opponent’s movement path rather than chase their current position.

Examples:

- cutting off cage movement
- stepping across retreat path
- passing ahead of hip rotation
- meeting a takedown entry before it reaches centre line

This converts prediction into positional advantage.

## Escaping the Line
Defensive movement often succeeds by leaving the incoming force path.

Examples:

- pivot
- lateral step
- angle change
- hip movement
- head movement

Moving slightly off-line may be cheaper than resisting force directly.

## Line and Distance
Distance changes along different lines.

A diagonal step may close distance faster to one target while increasing distance from another.

Synochi should therefore reason about distance directionally rather than only as absolute separation.

## Line and Footwork
Footwork determines whether the athlete can establish or escape a line while preserving base.

Poor footwork may create the correct angle but leave the athlete mechanically unable to act from it.

## Line and Force Direction
Force Direction describes where force travels.

Line of Attack describes the broader spatial pathway through which the offensive action develops.

They overlap but are not identical.

A strike may travel along one line while rotational force is generated through another.

## Line and Threat
A threat may exist because the attacker has established a strong line before the final action occurs.

Example:

Outside angle obtained  
→ shoulder line exposed  
→ opponent now vulnerable to takedown or strike

The geometric advantage precedes the technique.

## Predictive Value
Repeated line preferences can reveal tactical patterns.

Examples:

- opponent always exits clockwise
- athlete consistently attacks straight forward
- passer repeatedly enters on same diagonal
- wrestler repeatedly circles toward lead leg

This can allow Synochi to identify interceptable movement patterns.

## Diagnostic Example
Surface analysis:

"Your entry got stuffed."

Line-aware analysis:

"You attacked directly into his strongest defensive line. The outside step created the better route, but your hips never followed onto that new line."

## Coaching Value
Line of Attack and Line of Movement help Synochi explain:

- why angles matter
- why direct attacks stall
- how pivots create advantage
- how pressure should cut off movement
- how defensive movement can escape force
- why some entries become available only after repositioning

## Synochi Function
Synochi should reason about lines through:

**Identify orientation → Track movement path → Identify attack path → Compare lines → Detect intersection or divergence → Evaluate structural advantage → Observe outcome**

This should interact closely with footwork, angle, distance, force direction, base, and timing.

# Frames

## Type
Universal Concept

## Domain
[[Combat Intelligence Hub]]

## Definition
Frames are structural barriers created through skeletal alignment and contact that maintain space, redirect force, block movement, or prevent an opponent from collapsing distance.

A frame is not simply an arm or leg placed between two athletes.

It is a mechanically supported structure that changes how force travels through the exchange.

## Core Principle
Frames preserve structure by redirecting pressure into stronger skeletal pathways.

**Contact → Alignment → Structural support → Space preservation or force redirection**

A weak frame absorbs force muscularly.

A strong frame transfers force through connected structure.

## Why Frames Matter
Frames are central to:

- guard retention
- pin escape
- clinch defence
- takedown defence
- distance management
- posture recovery
- preventing chest-to-chest connection

They often determine whether pressure becomes control.

## Frame Components

### Contact Point
Where the frame meets the opponent.

Examples:

- forearm on shoulder
- hand on hip
- shin across torso
- elbow against knee
- head against chest

### Supporting Structure
The body segment carrying the force.

Examples:

- forearm connected to elbow
- elbow connected to rib cage
- shin connected through hip
- shoulder stacked behind arm

### Base Connection
The frame should connect back into the athlete’s larger structure.

Without support from torso, hips, or base, the frame may collapse.

## Long Frames
Long frames preserve distance.

Examples:

- extended arm against shoulder
- foot on hip
- shin frame

Advantages:

- maintain space
- slow forward pressure
- create room for movement

Risks:

- limb isolation
- overextension
- arm exposure

## Short Frames
Short frames operate closer to the body.

Examples:

- forearm across neck
- elbow-to-rib structure
- knee-elbow connection

Advantages:

- stronger structural support
- lower isolation risk
- useful under pressure

## Internal Frames
Some frames operate inside the athlete’s own structure.

Examples:

- elbow-knee connection
- forearm protecting neck
- knees maintaining internal space

These may not directly push the opponent but prevent structural collapse.

## External Frames
External frames directly contact and manage the opponent.

Examples:

- hand on shoulder
- shin across hip line
- forearm against collarbone

## Frame Direction
Frames work best when oriented against the incoming force.

A frame positioned poorly relative to pressure may collapse even if structurally strong.

Frame effectiveness therefore depends on:

- angle
- alignment
- contact position
- direction of pressure
- base support

## Frames and Distance
Frames regulate distance.

They can:

- maintain distance
- create space
- prevent connection
- delay pressure
- redirect approach

Loss of effective framing often allows the opponent to convert pressure into control.

## Frame Collapse
A frame may fail when:

- elbow separates from body
- joint angle becomes weak
- opponent redirects around it
- frame is overloaded
- base support disappears
- contact point slips
- opponent changes angle

## Frame Replacement
One frame may need to replace another as the exchange changes.

Example:

hand frame  
→ forearm frame  
→ knee frame  
→ guard recovery

Frame replacement creates continuity of structure.

## Frame Chains
Multiple frames can operate together.

Examples:

forearm + knee  
hand + shin  
elbow + hip frame

Combined frames can create a stronger barrier than one isolated structure.

## Frames and Wedges
Frames resist or redirect force.

Wedges occupy space and prevent structural movement.

The two often overlap but are not identical.

A frame can become a wedge when it prevents movement through fixed spatial occupation.

## Frames and Connection
Frames often regulate whether connection is allowed.

Defensively:

frame prevents chest connection.

Offensively:

frame may guide opponent into a predictable reaction.

Frames are therefore not purely defensive.

## Dynamic Framing
Frames should adapt during movement.

A static frame against changing pressure may become weak.

Effective framing involves:

- angle adjustment
- contact replacement
- posture adjustment
- connection to hip movement
- timing

## Frame Economy
Athletes should avoid using excessive muscular force where skeletal structure can do the work.

Efficient framing reduces energy cost.

Poor framing often creates:

- fatigue
- arm burnout
- delayed reactions
- collapsing posture

## Diagnostic Example
Surface analysis:

"Your frame collapsed."

Frame-aware analysis:

"Your forearm was in position, but your elbow drifted away from your ribs and lost structural support. Once that happened, the frame became muscular rather than skeletal."

## Coaching Value
Understanding frames helps Synochi diagnose:

- why pressure gets through
- why distance collapses
- why guard retention fails
- why escapes stall
- why athletes fatigue under pins
- which structure should be rebuilt first

## Synochi Function
Synochi should reason about frames through:

**Detect contact → Assess alignment → Identify structural support → Compare against incoming force → Track deformation → Detect collapse or replacement → Update space and constraint model**

Frames should interact with distance, wedges, posture, alignment, pressure, and recovery.

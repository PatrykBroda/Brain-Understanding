## **Core Idea**

Timing is the selection of the moment when an action has the greatest chance of succeeding.

An action can be technically correct and still fail if it is performed at the wrong moment.

Good timing means acting when:

- the opponent is moving,
- their structure is changing,
- their attention is occupied,
- their weight is committed,
- their defence is transitioning,
- or their available responses are reduced.

The central principle is:

**The same action can be weak or powerful depending on when it happens.**

## **Timing and Opportunity**

Timing depends on recognising windows.

A window may appear when the opponent:

- steps,
- strikes,
- reaches,
- posts,
- turns,
- changes level,
- shifts weight,
- resets posture,
- transitions position,
- or reacts to a threat.

These moments often create temporary vulnerability.

## **Static vs Dynamic Targets**

Attacking a static, prepared opponent is usually harder.

A moving opponent is already reorganising:

- base,
- posture,
- balance,
- attention,
- or defence.

That transition creates opportunity.

Good timing therefore often means attacking **during change**, not after the opponent has already stabilised.

## **Timing and Reaction**

Many actions become easier when performed during a predictable reaction.

For example:

**Threat → Reaction → Timed Follow-Up**

The athlete is not simply moving faster.

They are acting at the point where the opponent’s response has temporarily limited their options.

## **Timing and Distance**

[[Distance]] determines whether an action can physically reach or connect.

Timing determines whether the moment is favourable.

These must align.

An athlete may be at perfect range but attack while the opponent is fully prepared.

Or they may recognise the perfect moment but still be too far away to act.

Effective execution requires both.

## **Timing and Weight Transfer**

Weight shifts create important timing windows.

An opponent who has just committed weight may be:

- slower to reverse direction,
- vulnerable to off-balancing,
- unable to lift a loaded limb,
- or temporarily unable to reposition their base.

The timing of the attack may matter more than raw force.

## **Timing and Rhythm**

Repeated movement often creates rhythm.

Once a rhythm becomes predictable, timing can exploit it.

Examples include:

- entering between repeated strikes,
- attacking during a predictable reset,
- changing tempo to disrupt expectation,
- or acting just before the opponent completes a familiar sequence.

Good timing can therefore involve both reading rhythm and breaking rhythm.

## **Early vs Late Timing**

Timing errors often fall into two categories.

### **Too Early**

The opportunity has not fully formed.

The opponent still has:

- stable posture,
- available base,
- intact defence,
- or too much distance.

### **Too Late**

The opportunity has already closed.

The opponent has:

- recovered balance,
- rebuilt structure,
- reset distance,
- or completed their defensive reaction.

The effective window often exists between these points.

## **Defensive Timing**

Timing also governs defence.

A defensive action may be strongest:

- before the attack fully develops,
- during the opponent’s entry,
- at the point of contact,
- or during recovery.

Different defensive timings create different outcomes.

Early recognition can prevent the attack.

Later timing may require recovery rather than prevention.

## **Timing and Feints**

Feints can create timing windows by provoking premature reactions.

If an opponent reacts early:

- their guard may move,
- their weight may shift,
- their posture may change,
- or their attention may narrow.

The athlete can then attack the newly created window.

## **Timing Under Pressure**

Pressure can reduce timing quality by narrowing perception and increasing urgency.

An athlete may begin acting:

- too early,
- too late,
- or continuously without clear windows.

Strong timing requires enough perceptual control to recognise meaningful changes rather than reacting to everything.

## **Video Analysis**

FRAME should look for:

- whether the action itself was technically sound,
- whether the opportunity existed when it was attempted,
- whether the opponent was stable or transitioning,
- whether a better window appeared immediately before or after,
- and whether the athlete repeatedly misses the same timing cue.

A useful observation may be:

The entry was technically fine, but you attacked after they had already recovered their stance. The better window was during the previous weight shift.

Or:

You defended after the attack had fully developed instead of acting during the entry.

## **Coaching Implication**

Instead of only saying:

“Do it faster.”

FRAME should identify:

“Attack during the weight transfer.”

or:

“Enter as they reset their stance.”

This teaches the athlete to recognise the moment rather than simply increase speed.

## **Principle**

**Timing is the ability to act when the structure of the exchange is temporarily favourable.**

The best action is not only the correct action.

It is the correct action at the correct moment.

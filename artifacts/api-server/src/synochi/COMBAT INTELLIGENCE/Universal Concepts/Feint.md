# **Feint**

## **Core Idea**

A feint is a deliberately presented signal that suggests one action while preserving the ability to select another.

Its purpose is not deception for its own sake.

Its purpose is to create a predictable change in the opponent’s state.

A good feint can alter:

- attention,
- posture,
- guard,
- weight distribution,
- distance,
- timing,
- and option selection

before the real action begins.

The central sequence is:

**Signal → Prediction → Reaction → Exposure → Selection**

## **Feint as Signal Engineering**

A feint works by presenting information that the opponent interprets as meaningful.

The signal may resemble:

- an entry,
- a strike,
- a level change,
- a grip,
- a step,
- a posture shift,
- or another credible attack.

The opponent then updates their prediction of what is about to happen.

If the signal is believable, their system begins organising around that expected future state.

The feint exploits that reorganisation.

## **Credibility**

A feint only works if it resembles something real enough to require attention.

Credibility can come from:

- prior successful attacks,
- realistic movement,
- correct distance,
- matching posture,
- believable timing,
- and occasional reinforcement.

A signal with no consequence attached to it eventually loses value.

This links directly to [[Threat]].

## **Reaction Creation**

The true output of a feint is not the fake action.

It is the reaction it produces.

Examples include:

- hands rising,
- hands dropping,
- stance narrowing,
- weight shifting,
- a step backward,
- a level change,
- a defensive shell,
- a post,
- or a premature counter.

The useful question is:

What response am I trying to create?

Without that answer, the feint has no clear function.

## **Feint and Prediction**

Feints manipulate expectation.

The opponent sees a familiar signal and begins predicting the next state.

A successful feint creates:

**Expected state ≠ Actual state**

That mismatch produces a small prediction error.

The opponent must then reorient.

That reorientation costs time.

## **Feint and Timing**

[[Timing]] determines whether the athlete acts while the opponent is still committed to the false prediction.

If the athlete waits too long, the opponent re-stabilises.

The useful window often exists immediately after the reaction begins.

The sequence becomes:

**Feint → Reaction begins → Real action enters**

not:

**Feint → Wait → Attack**

## **Feint and Exposure**

A successful feint creates [[Exposure]].

The opponent reorganises protection around one expected threat and weakens another area.

This can create:

- structural exposure,
- perceptual exposure,
- or both.

The feint therefore acts as a way of manufacturing openings instead of waiting for them.

## **Feint and Option Compression**

A credible signal can narrow the opponent’s decision field.

If they believe one threat is imminent, their system may temporarily prioritise only a small number of defensive responses.

This reduces [[Option Density]].

The athlete gains an advantage by creating a more predictable response field.

## **Feint and Initiative**

A feint can establish [[Initiative]] without requiring heavy commitment.

The athlete presents the first meaningful problem.

The opponent reacts.

The athlete then selects from the reaction.

This is a low-cost way of gaining authorship over the exchange.

## **Feint Chains**

Feints can be chained.

For example:

**Feint A → reaction**

↓

**Feint B built from reaction**

↓

**Real action**

However, complexity only helps if each signal remains readable and credible.

Too many meaningless signals create noise rather than useful reaction.

## **Over-Feinting**

Feints lose value when overused.

If the opponent repeatedly sees the same signal without consequence, they update their model:

This signal does not require immediate response.

The feint stops creating meaningful state change.

This means deception must remain connected to genuine threat.

## **Defensive Feinting**

Feints can also be used defensively.

An athlete may present:

- apparent retreat,
- apparent vulnerability,
- an opening,
- or a false timing cue

to draw the opponent into a predictable action.

The athlete then acts on the commitment.

This converts defence into controlled invitation.

## **Perceptual Load**

Feints increase perceptual load.

The opponent must decide:

- Is this real?
- Do I respond?
- If I respond, how much?
- What else could follow?

Repeated uncertainty can slow clean selection.

The athlete who feints effectively is not only moving the opponent physically.

They are shaping what the opponent must process.

## **Video Analysis**

FRAME should look for:

- whether the feint actually produces a reaction,
- whether that reaction is consistent,
- whether the athlete acts while the reaction window is open,
- whether repeated feints have lost credibility,
- and whether the athlete is feinting without a clear intended response.

A useful observation may be:

Your level change consistently drew their hands down, but you attacked after they had already reset. The reaction was there — the timing of the follow-up was late.

Or:

The feint stopped working because it was shown repeatedly without ever being converted into a real attack.

## **Coaching Implication**

Instead of simply saying:

“Use more feints.”

FRAME should identify:

“Show the signal that makes them protect the lower line, then attack as that protection appears.”

The feint should always connect:

**signal → expected response → intended continuation**

## **Synochi Interpretation**

A feint is **controlled prediction manipulation**.

The athlete deliberately introduces a signal that causes the opponent’s system to commit resources toward an anticipated state.

When the anticipated state fails to occur, the opponent experiences a brief reorientation cost.

That cost is the opening.

The feint therefore works upstream of technique:

it alters the opponent’s model before it alters their body.

## **Principle**

**A good feint does not merely hide intention. It creates the reaction the athlete wants to act on.**

The value of the feint lies in the state change it causes, not in how convincing the fake looks.

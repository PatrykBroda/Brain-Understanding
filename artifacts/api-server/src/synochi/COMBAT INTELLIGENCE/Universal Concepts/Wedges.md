
## **Core Idea**

A wedge is a structure placed into space to prevent movement, redirect force, or preserve positional control.

In grappling, wedges often work better than muscular effort because they use:

- skeletal structure,
- body positioning,
- angle,
- pressure,
- and space occupation

to limit what the opponent can do.

The central principle is:

**A good wedge removes movement by occupying the space that movement requires.**

## **What a Wedge Does**

A wedge can:

- block rotation,
- stop an escape path,
- separate body segments,
- maintain distance,
- prevent reconnection,
- redirect force,
- or preserve an angle.

Examples include:

- a knee preventing hip rotation,
- a forearm blocking the shoulder line,
- a head preventing the opponent from turning,
- a shin maintaining separation,
- a hip controlling space between the opponent’s legs.

## **Structure Over Strength**

A wedge should ideally rely on structure rather than continuous muscular tension.

If the athlete has to squeeze constantly to maintain control, the position may be mechanically inefficient.

A well-placed wedge allows the skeleton and body mass to do more of the work.

This improves:

- control,
- endurance,
- stability,
- and responsiveness.

## **Space Occupation**

Many escapes require access to a specific piece of space.

A wedge works by occupying that space first.

For example:

If an opponent needs to bring their knee inside to recover guard, preventing that knee from entering the inside space can be more effective than reacting after guard recovery has already begun.

This makes wedges preventative rather than purely reactive.

## **Wedges and Constraint**

Wedges contribute directly to [[Constraint Accumulation]].

One wedge may remove one direction.

Several coordinated wedges can progressively reduce movement until only a small number of reactions remain.

For example:

- head blocks upper-body rotation,
- knee blocks hip movement,
- elbow prevents frame recovery.

Together, they create a much stronger control system than any single point alone.

## **Dynamic Wedges**

Wedges do not have to remain fixed.

They can move as the opponent moves.

A knee may become a shin.

A forearm may become an elbow frame.

A head wedge may shift to follow shoulder rotation.

The principle remains the same:

Occupy the space the opponent needs before they can effectively use it.

## **Offensive Wedges**

Offensively, wedges can help:

- isolate limbs,
- expose the back,
- prevent guard recovery,
- maintain pinning positions,
- reinforce submissions,
- and trap defensive structures.

A wedge often creates the stable platform from which the next attack becomes possible.

## **Defensive Wedges**

Defensively, wedges create separation and prevent connection.

Examples include:

- forearm frames,
- knee shields,
- elbow-knee connection,
- shin frames,
- head positioning.

A defensive wedge can stop pressure from fully transferring through the opponent’s body.

This can create the space required to move, rotate, or recover position.

## **Wedges and Connection**

Wedges and connection can appear opposite, but they often work together.

A wedge may create separation in one area while maintaining strong connection elsewhere.

For example:

A frame may keep the opponent’s shoulder away while the hips remain connected enough to control distance.

The important question is not simply:

Am I connected or separated?

It is:

Where should connection exist, and where should space be preserved?

## **Failure of a Wedge**

A wedge fails when:

- it is placed too shallow,
- the angle is poor,
- the opponent can move around it,
- it collapses under pressure,
- it relies too heavily on muscular effort,
- or it blocks the wrong space.

The presence of a limb does not automatically mean a functional wedge exists.

## **Video Analysis**

FRAME should look for:

- which spaces the athlete successfully occupies,
- which escape route remains open,
- whether a frame or wedge collapses,
- whether the athlete is using strength where structure could do the work,
- and whether lost position begins with removal of a critical wedge.

A useful observation may be:

You lost the pass when your knee stopped blocking the hip line. Once that wedge disappeared, they could rotate underneath you and recover guard.

## **Coaching Implication**

Instead of coaching only the final technique, FRAME should identify the missing structural barrier.

For example:

Instead of:

“Hold them tighter.”

Use:

“Keep your knee wedged against the hip so they can’t rotate underneath you.”

This makes the instruction more specific and mechanically useful.

## **Principle**

**Wedges control movement by controlling space.**

Strong grappling often feels efficient because the athlete is not constantly fighting movement after it begins.

They are occupying the space required for that movement before it becomes available.

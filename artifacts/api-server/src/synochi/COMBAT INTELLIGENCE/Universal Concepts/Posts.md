# Posts

## Type
Universal Concept

## Domain
[[Combat Intelligence Hub]]

## Definition
A post is a temporary structural contact used to support balance, prevent collapse, create distance, redirect force, or stabilise movement.

A post can be made through:

- hand
- forearm
- elbow
- foot
- knee
- head
- shoulder
- opponent contact
- floor or boundary contact

A post is not merely contact.

It becomes functionally meaningful when the athlete depends on it to preserve or redirect structure.

## Core Principle
Posts temporarily expand the athlete’s usable base.

**Loss of balance → Post created → Base restored or redirected**

Because of this, posts often reveal where balance is vulnerable.

## Why Posts Matter
Posts are central to:

- takedown defence
- scrambling
- guard retention
- sweeps
- technical stand-ups
- pin escapes
- striking balance
- wall wrestling

A post can be both a defensive necessity and an offensive opportunity.

## Post Function

### Balance Preservation
The athlete uses a post to stop falling or rotating.

### Base Expansion
The post increases the area supporting the athlete.

### Force Redirection
Incoming force is transferred through the post into the floor, opponent, or boundary.

### Distance Creation
A post can prevent an opponent from closing space.

### Transition Support
A temporary post may allow the athlete to move the hips, legs, or torso into a new configuration.

## Required vs Optional Posts
Synochi should distinguish between posts that are:

### Structurally Required
The athlete would likely lose balance or collapse if the post disappeared.

### Strategically Chosen
The post improves the position but is not necessary for survival.

### Incidental
The limb touches the surface without meaningful structural dependence.

This distinction matters because required posts are often attackable.

## Post Dependence
A post becomes increasingly important when:

- centre of mass moves outside the primary base
- stance narrows
- opponent creates rotational force
- another base point becomes unavailable
- posture is compromised

Synochi should therefore infer how dependent the athlete is on each post.

## Post Removal
Removing a required post can create immediate structural consequences.

Examples:

- sweep
- takedown
- collapse
- rotational exposure
- inability to defend another threat

This creates a common combat sequence:

**Force post → Remove post → Exploit imbalance**

## Forced Posting
An athlete can intentionally create a reaction that forces the opponent to post.

Examples:

- off-balancing
- snapdown
- sweep threat
- rotational pressure
- elevation

The post itself can then expose:

- arm
- back
- head
- angle
- leg

The initial action may therefore be valuable even if it does not complete the attack.

## Posting Direction
The location and direction of the post reveals the direction in which balance is threatened.

Example:

Right hand posts laterally.

This suggests the athlete’s structure is being displaced toward the right side.

Post direction can therefore provide evidence about:

- weight shift
- centre-of-mass movement
- rotational force
- off-balancing direction

## Post Timing
Early posts can stabilise structure efficiently.

Late posts may occur after balance has already deteriorated.

Synochi should distinguish:

**anticipatory post**

from

**emergency post**

## Post Vulnerability
A posting limb is often less available for other functions.

A hand supporting bodyweight may become temporarily unavailable for:

- grip fighting
- framing
- striking
- submission defence

This creates an option trade-off.

## Post Trapping
If a required post becomes trapped, the athlete’s ability to restore balance may collapse.

Examples:

- wrist ride
- hand trap
- leg trap
- elbow control

Post trapping can convert temporary instability into sustained control.

## Internal vs External Base
The athlete’s legs may form the primary base.

Posts create temporary external support beyond that base.

This distinction helps Synochi understand why removing one contact point can suddenly destabilise an apparently stable athlete.

## Dynamic Posting
Posts appear and disappear rapidly.

Synochi should track:

**balance disruption → post appearance → weight loading → post release → new base**

This sequence can reveal both defensive competence and offensive opportunity.

## Diagnostic Example
Surface analysis:

"He put his hand down."

Post-aware analysis:

"Your off-balance forced his right hand to the mat. Once that became weight-bearing, the arm was temporarily unavailable for defence and the back-side angle opened."

## Coaching Value
Post analysis helps Synochi identify:

- whether off-balancing is working
- where the opponent is structurally vulnerable
- which limb can be attacked or trapped
- why a sweep failed
- why an athlete repeatedly loses balance
- when an opponent has committed weight outside their base

## Synochi Function
Synochi should reason about posts through:

**Detect contact → Determine whether load-bearing → Infer balance dependency → Identify post direction → Detect post removal or trapping → Observe structural consequence**

Posts should interact with base, weight transfer, off-balancing, frames, timing, and exposure.

# Centre of Mass

## Type
Universal Concept

## Domain
[[Combat Intelligence Hub]]

## Definition
Centre of Mass is the effective point around which an athlete’s body mass is balanced and organised.

Its position relative to the athlete’s base, opponent, and direction of force strongly influences:

- balance
- mobility
- pressure
- stability
- weight transfer
- off-balancing
- ability to change direction

The centre of mass is not fixed.

It shifts continuously as posture, limb position, stance, and body orientation change.

## Core Principle
Balance depends on the relationship between mass and support.

**Centre of mass + Base + Momentum = Current stability**

The closer the centre of mass moves toward or beyond the limits of the usable base, the greater the demand for structural correction.

## Why It Matters
Many combat actions work by changing where mass is organised rather than directly overpowering the opponent.

Examples:

- pulling the head moves upper-body mass forward
- lifting a leg reduces the available base beneath the centre of mass
- rotating shoulders can shift mass outside the stance
- pressure passing moves mass through the opponent rather than simply toward them

## Centre of Mass and Base
A stable athlete generally keeps their centre of mass supported by their effective base.

If it moves outside that base, the athlete must respond through:

- stepping
- posting
- widening stance
- rotating
- lowering level
- creating new contact

This makes centre-of-mass displacement central to off-balancing.

## Effective Base
The effective base is not limited to the feet.

In grappling it may include:

- feet
- knees
- hands
- elbows
- hips
- opponent contact
- wall or cage

The same centre-of-mass position may therefore be stable in one configuration and unstable in another.

## Horizontal Displacement
Moving the centre of mass forward, backward, or laterally can create instability.

Examples:

- snapdown shifts mass forward
- arm drag shifts torso laterally
- push-pull sequences move mass across changing base boundaries

## Vertical Displacement
Changing height also affects mechanical behaviour.

Lower centre of mass may improve stability.

Higher centre of mass may improve mobility or elevation but can increase susceptibility to some off-balancing actions.

Vertical position should therefore be evaluated relative to task.

## Rotational Displacement
The body may remain spatially close to the same place while mass rotates around the base.

Examples:

- shoulder rotation against fixed hips
- turning opponent around a planted foot
- steering the upper body past the lower-body base

Rotational displacement can create instability without large linear movement.

## Centre of Mass Projection
Synochi should distinguish the approximate body centre from where that mass is projected relative to support.

Example:

An athlete leaning heavily forward may still have feet in the same location, but the projected mass has moved toward the front edge of the base.

This changes stability and available movement.

## Centre of Mass and Weight Distribution
Weight distribution reflects how the athlete’s mass is currently supported.

A centre-of-mass shift usually changes load across the available base.

Example:

Mass shifts right  
→ right leg becomes heavily loaded  
→ left leg becomes lighter  
→ left leg may become easier to attack

## Centre of Mass and Pressure
Pressure becomes mechanically effective when body mass is organised through a useful force path.

Example:

hips positioned correctly  
→ centre of mass projected through shoulder  
→ opponent receives sustained pressure

Pressure should therefore be understood partly as controlled centre-of-mass placement.

## Centre of Mass and Mobility
Stability and mobility exist in tension.

A deeply centred stance may be difficult to move but slower to reposition.

A mobile stance may allow rapid movement but create shorter windows of reduced stability.

Optimal positioning depends on the task.

## Centre of Mass and Momentum
Once mass is moving, stopping or redirecting it requires force.

An athlete can exploit this by adding force in the same direction as existing momentum.

Example:

opponent steps forward  
→ mass already travelling forward  
→ snapdown amplifies existing movement

## Centre of Mass Manipulation
An athlete can manipulate an opponent’s centre of mass through:

- head control
- underhooks
- grips
- frames
- pressure
- elevation
- angle changes
- push-pull actions

The objective is often not simply to move the body.

It is to move the body relative to its supporting structure.

## Centre of Mass Recovery
When displaced, the athlete can recover stability by reorganising support beneath the mass.

Examples:

- step under body
- post hand
- widen stance
- lower hips
- rotate base
- reconnect to opponent

## False Stability
An athlete may appear stationary while being structurally unstable.

If the centre of mass is near the edge of the base, only a small additional disturbance may force a post or step.

Synochi should therefore distinguish:

**not moving**

from

**mechanically stable**

## Diagnostic Example
Surface analysis:

"He was standing too tall."

Centre-of-mass analysis:

"His hips were high and his mass had shifted beyond his rear foot. Once you continued pulling forward, he had no base underneath the direction his weight was travelling."

## Coaching Value
Centre of Mass helps Synochi explain:

- why off-balancing works
- why posts appear
- why certain legs become light
- why pressure feels heavy
- why some stances are stable but immobile
- why movement becomes difficult after weight commitment
- when sweeps and takedowns become mechanically available

## Synochi Function
Synochi should reason about centre of mass through:

**Observe posture → Estimate mass position → Identify effective base → Compare mass projection with support → Track displacement → Predict required correction → Observe outcome**

Centre of Mass should serve as a foundational mechanical variable connecting balance, base, weight distribution, movement, pressure, and off-balancing.

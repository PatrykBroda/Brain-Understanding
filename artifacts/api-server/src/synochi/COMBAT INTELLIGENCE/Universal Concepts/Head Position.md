
## **Core Idea**

Head Position is the use of the head as an active point of control, structure, and directional influence.

The head is not simply carried along with the body.

In close-range grappling, it can function as:

- a frame,
- a wedge,
- a post,
- a steering point,
- a source of pressure,
- and a way to deny the opponent preferred movement.

Good head position can make the rest of the body stronger.

Poor head position can weaken otherwise correct grips and body position.

## **Head Position and Structure**

The head sits at the end of the spine.

Where the head goes affects:

- spinal alignment,
- posture,
- balance,
- shoulder position,
- hip direction,
- and the athlete’s ability to generate force.

If the opponent controls the head, they can often influence the rest of the body.

This is why head control frequently has an effect far greater than the size of the contact point suggests.

## **Winning Head Position**

Winning head position usually means placing the head where it:

- strengthens your own alignment,
- disrupts the opponent’s alignment,
- blocks a preferred direction,
- creates access to an attack,
- or prevents the opponent establishing their own control.

This can include:

- forehead under the jaw,
- temple-to-temple pressure,
- head inside the opponent’s head position,
- head against the chest or shoulder,
- using the skull to occupy space the opponent needs.

The exact position depends on the exchange.

## **Inside vs Outside Head Position**

Head position often interacts with inside position.

In many wrestling and clinch exchanges, inside head position can:

- improve body-lock access,
- strengthen underhooks,
- reduce the opponent’s turning ability,
- and make entries safer.

However, outside head position can also be correct depending on the attack.

The important question is not:

Is the head inside or outside?

It is:

What does this head position allow, deny, or expose in this specific exchange?

## **Directional Control**

The head can influence where the opponent is able to move.

For example:

A strong head position may block the opponent from turning toward one side.

This can force movement in the opposite direction.

The head therefore contributes to [[Constraint Accumulation]].

It can help narrow available reactions without relying entirely on grips.

## **Head Position in Takedowns**

During takedown entries, head position can affect:

- entry safety,
- posture,
- finishing angle,
- ability to drive,
- ability to turn the corner,
- and exposure to counters.

A technically correct shot can become weak if the head is badly placed.

Likewise, strong head position can make a relatively simple takedown difficult to stop.

## **Head Position in Passing**

When passing guard, the head can be used to:

- control upper-body direction,
- prevent the opponent turning in,
- pin the shoulders,
- reinforce crossface pressure,
- occupy defensive space,
- and help connect upper-body control to hip control.

The head often acts as an additional limb.

It can maintain control while the hands are required elsewhere.

## **Head Position in Pins**

In dominant positions, head position can help prevent:

- turning,
- bridging into space,
- recovering inside frames,
- or reconnecting the knees and elbows.

A strong pin often uses the head as part of a larger system of wedges and pressure rather than treating it independently.

## **Defensive Head Position**

Head position also matters defensively.

An athlete who allows their head to be turned, lifted, or displaced may lose:

- alignment,
- ability to bridge,
- ability to rotate,
- access to frames,
- or connection between upper and lower body.

Recovering head position can therefore be an important early step in escaping.

## **Head Position and Visibility**

Head placement also affects what the athlete can perceive.

Poor head position may:

- reduce visual information,
- turn the spine away from the action,
- create unnecessary exposure,
- or delay recognition of transitions.

Good head position should support both control and awareness.

## **Common Diagnostic Pattern**

A frequent technical error is:

Correct grips, poor head position.

The athlete may appear to have the right configuration but still lack control because the opponent’s head and spine remain free.

Another common error is:

Head follows the movement instead of leading or controlling it.

This makes the athlete structurally reactive.

## **Video Analysis**

FRAME should look for:

- who has dominant head position,
- whether the head supports the athlete’s grips,
- whether head position blocks or enables movement,
- whether the athlete loses alignment when the head is displaced,
- and whether a failed action can be traced back to poor head placement.

A useful observation may be:

Your underhook was present, but your head stayed outside their shoulder line, so they could turn back into you and neutralise the control.

## **Coaching Implication**

Coaching should treat head position as part of the technique rather than a minor detail.

Instead of only saying:

“Get the underhook.”

A stronger instruction may be:

“Get the underhook and win the head position so they can’t square back up.”

The head should contribute to the same directional objective as the rest of the body.

## **Principle**

**The head is a control surface.**

Good head position strengthens structure, shapes direction, and removes options.

In close-range grappling, losing the head battle often means losing more than just the head.

## **Core Idea**

Base is the structure that allows an athlete to support their body, resist displacement, and produce force without losing balance.

It is created by the relationship between:

- contact points,
- centre of mass,
- posture,
- weight distribution,
- and direction of force.

The stronger the base relative to the force being applied, the harder the athlete is to move.

## **Base Is Directional**

Base is not simply:

wide stance = strong.

An athlete can be very stable in one direction and weak in another.

A useful question is:

Where can this structure currently resist force, and where can it not?

This is why angle matters.

A stable opponent may become vulnerable as soon as force is redirected outside the direction their base can support.

## **Contact Points**

Base is created through contact with the floor or another supporting surface.

Examples include:

- feet,
- knees,
- hands,
- elbows,
- hips,
- shoulder,
- or combinations of these.

Adding a post can temporarily increase base.

Removing one can make the structure easier to disrupt.

## **Centre of Mass**

The centre of mass must remain sufficiently supported by the available base.

When it moves toward the boundary of that support, stability decreases.

If it moves beyond the available base, the athlete must:

- step,
- post,
- rotate,
- widen their structure,
- or fall.

This is the foundation of [[Off-Balancing]].

## **Active Base**

Base is not always static.

Athletes constantly rebuild it.

For example:

- stepping during wrestling,
- posting during a sweep,
- widening knees during a pin,
- placing a hand during a scramble.

Good grappling requires recognising both:

**the opponent’s current base**

and:

**how they are likely to rebuild it.**

## **Base Removal**

A powerful way to attack balance is to remove or occupy one of the structures the opponent needs for support.

Examples include:

- controlling a posting arm,
- trapping a foot,
- loading weight onto one leg,
- lifting a knee,
- controlling the hips,
- preventing a hand from reaching the floor.

Instead of overpowering the opponent’s entire structure, the athlete removes part of the structure supporting it.

## **Base and Posting**

A post is often an emergency expansion of base.

If an opponent is forced to post, that response creates information.

The post may:

- expose an arm,
- reduce defensive gripping,
- alter posture,
- open the back,
- or create a new attack.

Base therefore interacts directly with [[Reaction Mapping]].

## **Base in Standing**

Standing base depends heavily on:

- foot placement,
- stance width,
- weight distribution,
- knee position,
- hip position,
- and posture.

An athlete with both feet available and weight centred is harder to move than one whose weight has been committed to a single leg.

This makes weight transfer a major part of takedown setup.

## **Base in Ground Grappling**

Base still matters on the ground.

Examples include:

- a passer posting a hand to prevent being swept,
- a mounted athlete widening knees for stability,
- a bottom athlete using an elbow and foot to support a bridge,
- a turtle player using knees and elbows to maintain structure.

Ground positions can often be attacked by identifying which supporting point is essential and removing it.

## **Base and Mobility**

A larger base can increase stability but reduce mobility.

A narrower or lighter base may improve movement but decrease resistance to displacement.

Athletes constantly trade between:

**stability**

and:

**mobility.**

The correct base depends on the objective.

## **Base and Connection**

Strong [[Connection]] can allow an athlete to manipulate the opponent’s base more effectively.

If meaningful connection exists, movement of the attacker can transfer into the opponent’s centre.

Without connection, force may only move a limb while the opponent’s base remains intact.

## **Defensive Application**

When defending, rebuilding base can be more important than immediately attacking.

An athlete who has lost their base may need to:

- post,
- widen,
- reconnect to the floor,
- recover posture,
- or shift weight

before attempting a more complex action.

Trying to attack without sufficient base often creates further instability.

## **Video Analysis**

FRAME should look for:

- what currently supports each athlete,
- where the centre of mass sits relative to that support,
- whether weight becomes committed to one side,
- when a post becomes necessary,
- and whether an opportunity appears because a base point has been removed.

A useful observation may be:

You tried to finish the sweep while both of their posts were still available. Once you remove the far hand, their remaining base becomes much easier to collapse.

## **Coaching Implication**

Instead of only saying:

“Knock them over.”

FRAME can identify:

“Remove the post first.”

or:

“Shift their weight onto the leg you want to attack.”

This teaches the athlete to attack structure rather than rely on force.

## **Principle**

**Balance depends on support.**

To move an opponent efficiently, understand what is currently supporting them, where that support is weak, and what happens when one part of the base is removed.

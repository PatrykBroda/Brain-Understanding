## **Core Idea**

Distance is the spatial relationship between athletes that determines which actions are available, effective, or dangerous.

Every technique has a functional range.

If the athlete is too far away, the action may not reach.

If the athlete is too close, the action may lose structure, power, or timing.

Combat therefore depends heavily on controlling:

**when distance opens, when it closes, and who benefits from that change.**

## **Functional Range**

Distance should not be understood only as physical space.

It should be understood as:

What can each athlete successfully do from here?

Different ranges favour different actions.

Examples include:

- long striking range,
- punching range,
- kicking range,
- clinch range,
- takedown range,
- entanglement range,
- ground-control range.

The exact expression changes between sports, but the principle remains the same.

## **Distance Is Relative**

A distance that is safe for one athlete may be dangerous for another.

This depends on:

- reach,
- stance,
- speed,
- available techniques,
- ruleset,
- posture,
- angle,
- and reaction time.

The useful question is not:

How far apart are they?

It is:

Who currently has effective access?

## **Entry Distance**

Many attacks require the athlete to first cross distance.

An entry succeeds when the athlete can close enough space to act without allowing the opponent an easier response.

This applies to:

- punches,
- kicks,
- takedowns,
- clinch entries,
- submissions,
- positional transitions.

Good entries change distance while preserving structure and reducing exposure.

## **Exit Distance**

After attacking, the athlete may need to create distance again.

A successful attack can still create danger if the athlete remains in the opponent’s preferred range.

Exit distance therefore matters as much as entry distance.

The sequence may be:

**Enter → Act → Exit**

or:

**Enter → Connect → Transition**

depending on the objective.

## **Distance Control**

Distance can be controlled through:

- footwork,
- stance,
- frames,
- head position,
- grips,
- posts,
- pressure,
- feints,
- movement,
- and positional barriers.

Control does not always mean keeping the opponent away.

Sometimes the athlete wants to deny separation.

Sometimes they want to deny entry.

The objective determines the preferred distance.

## **Closing Distance**

Closing distance can remove certain weapons while enabling others.

For example:

A long-range striker may become less effective once an opponent enters clinch range.

Likewise, a grappler may be unable to initiate meaningful control until sufficient connection is established.

Distance changes the available tactical menu.

## **Creating Distance**

Creating distance can:

- reset an exchange,
- remove immediate threat,
- create striking space,
- restore defensive structure,
- improve visibility,
- or force the opponent to re-enter.

Distance creation is therefore not always retreat.

It can be a deliberate tactical reset.

## **Distance and Timing**

Correct distance without correct timing may still fail.

An athlete may enter the right range at the wrong moment and meet resistance.

Likewise, excellent timing cannot compensate for being physically unable to reach the target.

Distance and timing therefore interact continuously.

## **Distance and Angle**

Changing angle also changes effective distance.

A target may become closer or farther without either athlete moving directly forward or backward.

An angle can:

- shorten the path to the target,
- lengthen the opponent’s return path,
- expose a side,
- or remove a defensive barrier.

Distance should therefore be understood three-dimensionally rather than only as straight-line separation.

## **Distance as Information**

Distance also communicates intent.

When an opponent suddenly:

- steps closer,
- retreats,
- changes stance,
- lowers level,
- or shifts weight,

they may be preparing to enter or leave a particular range.

Recognising these changes allows earlier decision-making.

## **Video Analysis**

FRAME should look for:

- whether the athlete acts from functional range,
- whether attacks begin too far away or too close,
- whether the athlete enters safely,
- whether they remain in danger after attacking,
- and whether distance changes favour one athlete repeatedly.

A useful observation may be:

The technique itself was available, but you initiated from outside effective range, which gave the opponent enough time to recognise and react.

Or:

You landed the exchange but stayed inside their preferred range afterward instead of resetting.

## **Coaching Implication**

Instead of only correcting technique execution, FRAME should ask:

Was the athlete at the correct distance for the technique to work?

A technically sound action performed from the wrong range may fail consistently.

Distance problems should therefore be separated from technique problems.

## **Principle**

**Combat actions only exist inside usable range.**

Strong athletes do not simply know techniques.

They understand how to enter, maintain, manipulate, and exit the distances where those techniques become effective.

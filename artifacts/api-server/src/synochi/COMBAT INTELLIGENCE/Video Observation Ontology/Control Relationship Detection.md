## Definition

Control Relationship Detection identifies who currently has meaningful control within an exchange and what mechanisms are producing that control.

## Core Principle

Being on top does not automatically mean being in control.

Control should be inferred from functional constraints placed on the opponent.

## Observable Control Mechanisms

Examples include:

- Head control
- Hip control
- Shoulder control
- Inside position
- Underhooks
- Overhooks
- Frames
- Hooks
- Leg entanglement
- Wrist control
- Body locks
- Crossfaces
- Wedges
- Pinning pressure
- Grip dominance
- Posture disruption
- Base disruption
- Limb isolation

## Control Questions

FRAME should ask:

- Who can move freely?
- Who is being constrained?
- Who is dictating direction?
- Who controls inside space?
- Who controls the opponent's posture?
- Who controls the hips or shoulders?
- Who has meaningful attacking options?
- Who is reacting rather than initiating?

## Control Is Graduated

Control should not be treated as binary.

Possible states include:

- No clear control
- Slight initiative
- Temporary control
- Stable control
- Dominant control
- Submission-level control

## Local vs Global Control

An athlete may control one part of the body while losing the larger positional battle.

Example:

An athlete may control a wrist while their opponent controls their hips, head and passing direction.

FRAME should distinguish local control from overall control.

## Dynamic Control

Control may change rapidly.

The system should recognise moments where control migrates from one athlete to the other.

This connects directly to [[Control Migration]].

## Coaching Relevance

Control detection helps FRAME identify:

- Why a pass succeeds
- Why guard retention fails
- Why escapes stall
- Why submission entries become available
- Which athlete is dictating the exchange

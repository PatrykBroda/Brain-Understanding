# **Origins of Judo**

Judo was developed in Japan in the late nineteenth century by Jigoro Kano.

Kano had studied several traditional jujutsu systems and became interested not only in preserving effective techniques, but in reorganising them into a system that could be practised repeatedly, taught systematically and tested against resistance.

In 1882, Kano founded the Kodokan in Tokyo.

This marked a major shift.

Rather than treating martial technique as a collection of inherited forms, Kano began building a structured system around:

- technical efficiency
- balance disruption
- timing
- leverage
- live resistance
- repeatable training
- progressive learning
- safe but meaningful competition

The system became known as Judo.

## **From Jujutsu to Judo**

Traditional Japanese jujutsu was not a single unified martial art.

It consisted of multiple schools with different technical emphases, including:

- throws
- joint locks
- pins
- strangles
- weapons defence
- striking
- battlefield-oriented techniques

Kano studied several of these traditions, particularly Tenjin Shinyo Ryu and Kito Ryu.

He did not simply copy them.

He selected, modified and reorganised techniques according to a broader training philosophy.

One of the most important changes was the increased emphasis on techniques that could be practised repeatedly against resisting partners without producing unacceptable injury.

This transformed the training environment.

Instead of relying predominantly on compliant repetition or prearranged forms, students could engage in live practice.

This became randori.

## **Randori and the Importance of Resistance**

Randori allowed practitioners to attempt techniques against opponents who were actively trying to stop them.

This had enormous consequences.

A technique could now be evaluated through repeated exposure to:

- timing
- resistance
- fatigue
- failed entries
- counters
- positional recovery
- opponent adaptation

The training environment itself became a selection mechanism.

Techniques that could be safely practised under resistance could be refined thousands of times.

Techniques that relied on compliance, surprise or excessive injury risk became harder to test in the same way.

This created a fundamental principle that still shapes modern combat sport:

**What can be repeatedly tested under resistance can evolve rapidly.**

For FRAME, this is one of the most useful pieces of historical context within Judo.

The importance of Judo is not only that it produced particular throws.

It helped formalise a training architecture in which technical knowledge could continuously encounter reality.

## **Seiryoku Zenyo**

A central idea associated with Kano was **Seiryoku Zenyo**.

It is commonly translated as something close to:

**maximum efficient use of energy**

or

**best use of physical and mental energy**

The principle is often misunderstood as simply using less strength.

It is broader than that.

Efficiency can involve:

- applying force in the correct direction
- attacking at the correct moment
- creating imbalance before committing
- using structure instead of unnecessary muscular effort
- coordinating multiple parts of the body
- avoiding effort that does not contribute to the intended outcome

This principle still maps directly onto modern grappling.

A technically efficient movement reduces the amount of force required to create a result.

That does not mean strength is irrelevant.

It means strength becomes more useful when organised through position, timing and structure.

## **Kuzushi**

One of the most important concepts inherited from Judo is **kuzushi**.

Kuzushi refers to the disruption of an opponent’s balance or structural stability.

A common simplified model of a throw is:

**Kuzushi → Tsukuri → Kake**

### **Kuzushi**

Create imbalance or compromise the opponent’s ability to maintain structure.

### **Tsukuri**

Position yourself appropriately for the technique.

### **Kake**

Complete the execution.

This model is historically associated with throwing, but its usefulness extends far beyond throws.

In modern grappling, kuzushi can occur through:

- pulling
- pushing
- rotational force
- elevation
- compression
- removing posts
- controlling the head
- controlling the hips
- forcing weight onto one limb
- creating conflicting directional demands

From a Synochi perspective, kuzushi can be interpreted more broadly as:

**creating a structural problem that forces adaptation.**

This makes it relevant to:

- sweeps
- guard passing
- takedowns
- back takes
- pin escapes
- submission entries
- positional transitions

The same underlying principle appears in different technical environments.

## **Judo Competition and Technical Selection**

As Judo developed into a competitive sport, its rules increasingly shaped its technical landscape.

Throws became highly rewarded.

Pins, strangles and certain joint locks remained part of the system, but competition structure placed significant value on decisive standing attacks.

Over time, rule changes influenced which behaviours became more common.

Changes to gripping rules, leg attacks, scoring and groundwork duration all altered the strategic environment.

This is important because technical systems do not evolve independently of their rules.

A ruleset creates a selective environment.

The athlete adapts to what the environment rewards.

This can produce technical specialisation that may be highly effective inside one ruleset but less transferable outside it.

FRAME should therefore distinguish between:

**technical effectiveness**

and

**ruleset effectiveness**

A technique can be excellent within the conditions that selected for it while requiring modification in another environment.

## **Newaza**

Judo also contains a substantial groundwork component known as **newaza**.

Historically, groundwork played a major role in the development of Judo, particularly through exchanges with schools such as Fusen Ryu and through competitive experimentation.

Newaza includes:

- pins
- strangles
- joint locks
- turnovers
- positional transitions

As Judo competition evolved, groundwork became increasingly constrained by rules and refereeing expectations.

This created a different developmental path from Brazilian Jiu Jitsu.

BJJ competition generally allowed extended ground engagement.

Judo increasingly encouraged rapid progress on the ground before competitors were returned to standing.

The same ancestral technical environment therefore split into different strategic ecosystems.

One branch increasingly rewarded prolonged positional development.

The other increasingly rewarded rapid progression toward a decisive outcome.

This distinction helps explain why BJJ and Judo can share techniques while expressing them differently.

## **The Kano Model of Technical Development**

Kano’s deeper contribution can be understood as a model of system design.

He took inherited knowledge and placed it inside a structure that allowed:

**Practice → Resistance → Feedback → Refinement**

This dramatically increased the ability of practitioners to discover what worked under pressure.

The system did not depend solely on historical authority.

It created conditions in which the environment itself could provide feedback.

This idea is directly relevant to FRAME.

FRAME should not treat coaching knowledge as a static library of correct techniques.

A more powerful system is:

**recommendation → athlete attempts → outcome observed → response interpreted → future recommendation updated**

This mirrors the same underlying principle that made randori so powerful.

Knowledge improves when it encounters resistance.

## **Judo and Modern Grappling**

Judo remains a major technical ancestor of modern grappling.

Its influence can be seen in:

- throwing mechanics
- gripping systems
- balance disruption
- foot sweeps
- hip throws
- sacrifice throws
- pins
- strangles
- arm locks
- transition speed
- positional pressure

Modern BJJ frequently uses Judo techniques while adapting them to different stance, gripping and ruleset conditions.

No Gi grappling further modifies many of these techniques because traditional jacket grips are unavailable.

This means FRAME should avoid treating a technique name as a fixed object.

For example:

**the same underlying throw can change substantially depending on:**

- Gi or No Gi
- stance
- grip availability
- wrestling threat
- submission threat
- boundary conditions
- scoring incentives

The historical technique remains useful, but its modern expression is environment-dependent.

## **Synochi Interpretation**

Judo provides several concepts that remain highly useful for modern coaching.

### **1. Resistance Creates Information**

A technique tested repeatedly against resistance produces richer information than one understood only theoretically.

FRAME can use this principle when evaluating training.

A failed technique is not simply a negative outcome.

It is information about:

- timing
- positioning
- opponent reaction
- structural weakness
- decision quality

### **2. Efficiency Is Contextual**

Seiryoku Zenyo suggests that good technique is not merely low effort.

It is efficient organisation of effort.

FRAME should therefore distinguish between:

**necessary force**

and

**wasted force**

The goal is not always relaxation.

The goal is correct allocation of effort.

### **3. Create the Problem Before Solving It**

Kuzushi shows that successful execution often begins before the visible technique.

The athlete first creates a condition in which the opponent becomes easier to move.

FRAME can therefore coach athletes to ask:

**What reaction or structural failure needs to exist before this technique becomes available?**

### **4. Rules Shape Behaviour**

Judo demonstrates how strongly competitive environments shape technical evolution.

FRAME should always interpret performance relative to the ruleset.

A behaviour that looks inefficient in one sport may be highly rational in another.

### **5. Training Systems Shape Technical Evolution**

Randori did more than improve individual athletes.

It created an environment where the martial art itself could evolve.

This principle can be generalised:

**The quality of the feedback environment determines the quality of adaptation.**

## **FRAME Coaching Use**

Historical Judo context should be pulled when it helps FRAME explain modern performance.

Examples include:

- explaining why a throw failed because kuzushi never occurred
- identifying excessive muscular effort where structure could do more work
- distinguishing Gi gripping solutions from No Gi adaptations
- explaining why Judo athletes transition rapidly on the ground
- identifying when a ruleset has shaped a technical habit
- designing drills that preserve live resistance instead of becoming purely cooperative
- helping an athlete understand why creating a reaction is often more important than forcing the final technique

The historical value of Judo is therefore not simply that it came before modern grappling.

Its deeper value is that it provides one of the clearest early examples of a combat system deliberately designed around:

**efficiency, resistance, feedback and adaptation.**

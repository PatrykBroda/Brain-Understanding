
## **Core Idea**

Reaction Mapping is the process of identifying how an opponent responds to a threat and using that response to predict the next available action.

The central idea is:

**Threats create reactions. Reactions create openings.**

A technique should therefore not be understood only by its direct objective.

It should also be understood by the reactions it reliably produces.

## **Reaction Structure**

For any meaningful attack or positional threat, Combat Intelligence should ask:

- What reaction is this likely to create?
- What direction does the opponent move?
- What posture changes?
- What frames or grips appear?
- What weight shifts?
- What limbs become exposed?
- What new positions become available?

This creates a map from:

**Threat → Reaction → Opportunity**

## **Example**

An athlete begins passing to one side.

The opponent reacts by:

- turning their hips,
- inserting a frame,
- exposing an underhook,
- or shifting weight away from the passing direction.

The correct next action may depend less on forcing the original pass and more on recognising which reaction occurred.

The reaction determines the next branch.

## **Preferred Reactions**

Athletes often defend in recurring ways.

One opponent may consistently:

- turn away,
- frame hard,
- turtle,
- square back up,
- retreat,
- overcommit an underhook,
- or expose the legs.

These tendencies become useful tactical information.

If a reaction repeats, the athlete can begin attacking the reaction rather than merely attacking the position.

## **Reaction Chains**

Reactions can also be chained.

For example:

**Threat A → Reaction A → Threat B → Reaction B → Finish**

The athlete is no longer hoping one technique works.

They are guiding the opponent through a sequence of increasingly constrained choices.

## **Forced vs Optional Reactions**

Not all reactions are equal.

Some are:

**Forced reactions**  
The opponent has very few realistic alternatives.

Others are:

**Preferred reactions**  
The opponent has options but repeatedly chooses one.

Others are:

**Unpredictable reactions**  
The situation remains too open to anticipate reliably.

Strong systems tend to create more forced or highly probable reactions.

## **Video Analysis**

Reaction Mapping is especially useful for video analysis.

FRAME should look for moments where:

- an athlete successfully creates a reaction,
- the reaction exposes something useful,
- but the athlete fails to recognise or exploit it.

This may indicate that the athlete’s problem is not technical execution.

It may be:

poor reaction recognition.

Or:

insufficient connection between one attack and the next.

## **Coaching Implication**

Instead of teaching:

“Do this technique.”

Coaching can become:

“Use this threat to create this reaction. When they give you that reaction, move to the next action.”

This creates more adaptive and transferable skill.

## **Principle**

**The opponent’s reaction is part of the technique.**

A complete attack should include not only:

**what I do**

but also:

**what they are likely to do because of it**

and:

**what I do next.**

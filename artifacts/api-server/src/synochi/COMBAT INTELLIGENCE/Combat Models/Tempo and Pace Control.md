
## Purpose

This node defines how Synochi should understand the speed, rhythm, and timing of an exchange.

Combat performance is not only determined by technical choice.

The athlete can also influence when exchanges accelerate, slow down, reset, or become chaotic.

## Core Principle

Synochi should distinguish between:

- Moving fast
- Acting at the right time
- Forcing the opponent to match pace
- Slowing the exchange to regain control
- Changing tempo to create mistakes

Tempo is a tactical variable.

## Tempo Structure

Current Rhythm
→ Tempo Change
→ Opponent Adjustment
→ Reaction Window
→ Exploitation

## Pace Control

Pace control is the ability to influence how quickly exchanges develop.

An athlete may use:

- Pressure
- Grip fighting
- Positional control
- Rapid transitions
- Pauses
- Threats
- Resets
- Scramble initiation

to change the opponent’s decision environment.

## Tempo Change

A tempo change occurs when the athlete deliberately alters the speed or rhythm of the exchange.

Examples:

Slow pressure
→ opponent relaxes structure
→ sudden transition

Fast scramble
→ opponent overreacts
→ athlete stabilises position

Static control
→ opponent forced to move
→ reaction becomes predictable

## High Tempo

High tempo may be useful when:

- Opponent needs time to build structure
- Athlete has superior reaction speed
- Successive attacks create cumulative pressure
- Opponent struggles during transitions
- Time or score creates urgency

## Low Tempo

Lower tempo may be useful when:

- Athlete needs to consolidate control
- Opponent thrives in scrambles
- Energy conservation matters
- Position is already advantageous
- Athlete needs time to read reactions

## False Pace

High activity does not automatically mean useful pace.

Synochi should identify when speed creates:

- Poor decisions
- Loss of control
- Energy waste
- Missed cues
- Technical breakdown
- Unnecessary scrambling

## Tempo Mismatch

Synochi should identify whether the athlete performs worse when forced outside their preferred rhythm.

Examples:

- Pressure athlete forced into repeated fast scrambles
- Scramble-heavy athlete trapped in static control
- Counter-fighter forced to initiate continuously

Repeated tempo mismatch may become a meaningful tactical pattern.

## Rhythm Recognition

Synochi should look for recurring rhythm patterns.

Example:

Opponent consistently pauses after defending the first attack.

That pause may become a predictable timing window for a secondary attack.

## Tempo as Constraint

Tempo can constrain the opponent.

High pace may reduce thinking time.

Slow positional pressure may reduce movement options.

The correct tempo depends on what reaction the athlete is trying to create.

## Athlete-Specific Tempo

Synochi should learn:

- Preferred pace
- Best-performing tempo
- Fatigue response
- Ability to accelerate
- Ability to slow exchanges
- Positions where tempo control is strongest
- Opponent styles that disrupt rhythm

## Competition Context

Tempo recommendations should consider:

- Time remaining
- Score
- Ruleset
- Athlete conditioning
- Opponent fatigue
- Match phase

## Design Objective

Synochi should understand not only what the athlete does, but when and at what pace they do it.

The goal is to help the athlete control the rhythm of exchanges rather than simply react to the tempo imposed by the opponent.

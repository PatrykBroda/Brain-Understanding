
## Purpose

This node defines how Synochi should account for the physical cost of combat actions.

Two actions may produce similar outcomes while requiring very different amounts of energy.

## Core Principle

Combat efficiency matters.

Synochi should ask:

“What does this action cost relative to what it produces?”

## Energy Cost Factors

Synochi should consider:

- Muscular effort
- Isometric tension
- Explosive demand
- Repeated failed attempts
- Grip fatigue
- Scramble intensity
- Positional pressure required
- Recovery requirement
- Opponent resistance

## High-Cost Patterns

Examples include:

- Repeated explosive bridging
- Forcing submissions without isolation
- Holding inefficient grips
- Constant failed takedown attempts
- Maintaining unstable positions through strength
- Repeated scrambles caused by poor control

## Efficient Action

An efficient action produces useful advantage with relatively low unnecessary physical cost.

Efficiency may come from:

- Better structure
- Better timing
- Better leverage
- More predictable reaction creation
- Greater positional control
- Cleaner decision-making

## Energy Debt

Synochi should recognise when repeated high-cost actions create downstream performance decline.

Example:

Repeated failed shots
→ fatigue
→ slower reactions
→ weaker defence
→ positional loss

The original issue may therefore have both technical and energetic consequences.

## Tactical Energy Use

High energy expenditure is not always bad.

It may be justified when:

- Finish probability is high
- Match time is short
- Scoring opportunity is significant
- Opponent is close to breaking
- Competition context demands urgency

## Athlete-Specific Efficiency

Synochi should learn:

- Which positions drain the athlete
- Which systems they can sustain
- Where they recover well
- Which techniques repeatedly create unnecessary energy cost

## Design Objective

Synochi should help the athlete produce more useful combat output per unit of effort.

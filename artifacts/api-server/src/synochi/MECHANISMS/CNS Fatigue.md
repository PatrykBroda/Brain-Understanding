## Definition
CNS Fatigue refers to a reduction in the nervous system’s ability or willingness to produce high levels of motor output after sufficient training stress, sleep disruption, accumulated load, psychological stress, or inadequate recovery.

Within Synochi, the term should be treated as a practical performance-state label rather than a precise standalone diagnosis.

It is useful when an athlete reports or displays:

- unusually poor explosiveness
- reduced coordination
- slower reaction quality
- flatness despite adequate motivation
- reduced force production
- difficulty recruiting intensity
- unusually high effort for normally manageable work

## Core Principle
The important question is not:

“Is the athlete definitely experiencing CNS fatigue?”

The useful question is:

“Does the athlete currently show a pattern of reduced neural performance that changes what training intervention makes sense?”

Synochi should use CNS Fatigue as a hypothesis that becomes more or less plausible when combined with other evidence.

## Common Contributing Factors
Possible contributors include:

- high-intensity training accumulation
- repeated maximal or near-maximal efforts
- insufficient sleep
- disrupted [[NREM Sleep]]
- inadequate recovery between sessions
- psychological stress
- high competition load
- illness or general systemic fatigue
- excessive training monotony
- caloric or hydration deficits

No single factor should automatically be treated as causal.

## Observable Signals
Potential signals include:

- reduced speed despite effort
- loss of sharpness
- poor timing
- technical deterioration under low levels of fatigue
- unusually slow warm-up
- difficulty producing maximal intent
- reduced willingness to engage explosively
- coordination errors not normally present
- repeated reports of feeling “flat” or “heavy”

These signals are more useful when they represent a change from the athlete’s own baseline.

## Distinguish From Local Muscular Fatigue
CNS Fatigue should not be used as a catch-all for general tiredness.

Local muscular fatigue may present as:

- specific muscle soreness
- local loss of endurance
- burning or heaviness in one area
- technique failure linked clearly to local fatigue

A broader neural-fatigue pattern is more likely when performance degradation appears across multiple movements or tasks.

## Interaction With Sleep
Poor or disrupted sleep may increase the likelihood of reduced neural performance.

[[NREM Sleep]] is particularly relevant because deep sleep is associated with restorative processes and recovery from training stress.

Synochi should not infer:

poor NREM sleep = CNS fatigue

Instead, use sleep as one contextual signal among several.

## Coaching Implications
When CNS Fatigue appears plausible, FRAME should consider modifying the training objective rather than simply telling the athlete to stop.

Possible interventions include:

- reduce intensity
- reduce high-output rounds
- prioritise technical work
- shorten the session
- increase rest between efforts
- shift toward lower-complexity tasks
- prioritise recovery before the next high-intensity exposure

The correct intervention depends on severity, recent load, upcoming competition demands, and the athlete’s response history.

## Coaching Preference
FRAME should avoid over-diagnosing fatigue from one bad session.

Prefer:

observe performance change
→ compare against baseline
→ inspect recent load and sleep
→ identify supporting or conflicting signals
→ select conservative intervention
→ reassess

If the athlete performs normally after warming up, the system should reduce confidence in a CNS Fatigue interpretation.

## Competition Context
Near competition, CNS Fatigue becomes especially relevant because additional hard training may produce little adaptation while reducing performance readiness.

Synochi should therefore weigh:

- proximity to competition
- recent hard-session density
- sleep quality
- perceived readiness
- movement quality
- explosive performance

The closer the athlete is to competition, the lower the tolerance for unnecessary fatigue accumulation.

## Video and Session Analysis
FRAME may infer possible neural fatigue when repeated footage shows:

- slower initiation
- reduced scramble speed
- poorer positional reactions
- coordination errors
- delayed responses
- unusually weak explosive actions

Video alone is insufficient.

The strongest interpretation comes from combining footage with recent training load, sleep, self-report, and prior baseline.

## Failure Mode
A major system error would be treating every poor performance as CNS Fatigue.

Alternative explanations may include:

- technical uncertainty
- low motivation
- local muscular fatigue
- poor nutrition
- anxiety
- injury
- unfamiliar training partners
- deliberate pacing
- normal session variability

Synochi should maintain competing interpretations until evidence becomes stronger.

## Synochi Interpretation
CNS Fatigue is a state-estimation problem.

The system should ask:

Is the athlete currently showing a meaningful reduction in neural performance capacity, and does that change the best intervention?

The useful logic is:

detect performance drop
→ compare with baseline
→ inspect recovery context
→ maintain competing explanations
→ adjust training demand if warranted
→ observe response
→ update confidence
